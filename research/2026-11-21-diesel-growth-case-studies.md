# Diesel and truck build growth case studies: how 12 accounts went from nothing to 20K to 500K, and a 30-post plan for @elcoyotediesel

Research run 2026-11-21 for @elcoyotediesel (577 followers, Creator account, Cheyenne WY, 1959 Ford F600 on a Super Duty frame with a built 7.3 Powerstroke, Road to SEMA 2027). None of the 12 accounts below appear in `2026-11-21-profile-conversion.md` section 1 or `2026-11-21-build-series-structure.md` section 1. Zac Lucarz was a one-line mention in the series file; he gets a full reconstruction here because his captions date his own milestones.

## 0. Method and what could and could not be measured

- **TikTok**: `yt-dlp --flat-playlist` on a channel URL returns the complete post history with upload date, views, likes, comments, shares and duration. Every TikTok account below was dumped in full (14,000 posts total) and the "first 30 posts" are the oldest 30 in that dump. TikTok follower counts are NOT exposed logged out (profile pages return a blank shell, yt-dlp returns NA for `channel_follower_count`). Where a TikTok follower number appears it comes from a third-party list (ASE Connects, Jan 2026) or the creator's own caption.
- **Instagram**: logged-out profile fetch gives name field, bio, follower count, link, highlights and the first 12 tiles. About half the handles returned an image-only shell today (jeff.salv, desertdieselscartel, thedieselqueen, dieseltechchris, greg_alberalla). No follower history exists anywhere public: Social Blade returns 403 to automated fetches.
- **YouTube**: channel dumps give views and titles; dates had to be pulled video by video, so only the episodes that matter were dated.
- **Milestones**: dated only when a caption, a press piece or a platform number says so. "Undated" means exactly that. Views-by-quarter medians are used as the growth proxy where no follower number exists; a step change in median views is the closest public signal to a step change in followers.
- Likes, comments and shares are today's lifetime numbers for the post, not what they were at the time.

Scratch data for every dump lives in the session scratchpad (`tt/*.tsv`, `tt/sum_*.txt`, `tt/win_*.txt`) and will not survive the session. The numbers below are the extract.

## 4. The first 30 posts: what the fastest growers posted, in order

The three accounts that cleared 100K inside two months (Robert, Jeff, gearman) and the two that ramped hardest later (Blake, Zac in May 2023) share a shape. The slow starters (Konner, Chris, Caylee, DDC) share the opposite one.

**Fast shape**
1. Posts 1 to 10: the premise is already the post. Robert roasts a submitted truck in post 1 and writes "send in yours" in the caption. Jeff's post 2 is a junkyard dentside with his boots in frame. Gearman's post 11 is a Kenworth transmission and he never posts anything else again. Blake's post 2 is "Would you rock this setup?"
2. Posts 10 to 30: one format, repeated, with the caption doing a job: a question ("Which one are you choosing?"), a dare ("Name a cleaner two tone truck, I'll wait"), a number ("2 trucks for $500", "only had it 15 days"), or an instruction ("DM yours if you want to be next").
3. Somewhere in posts 15 to 70 a REACTION post lands: a stitch (gearman 15, Robert's duet with @texasgarage), a greenscreen on someone else's truck (Robert 70), a reply to a comment (Robert 24, "Replying to @derrick.lanham brb currently crying", 30K at post 24 was his first above-median post), a hater line (Blake 32, Konner 145).
4. Density: 5 days (Robert), 70 days (gearman), 90 days (Blake's Q4 2021 ramp) to reach 30 posts, then 2 to 6 a day for a quarter after the hit.
5. The person is audible or visible from post 1: Robert's voice, Blake's shop, Chloe's face, Caylee's narration. Gearman is the exception (hands and parts only) and he compensates with talking.

**Slow shape**
1. Posts 1 to 30 are the owner's one truck, shot from the driveway, captioned with hashtags only (Konner: 28 of 30; Chris: most of 150; Tyler: all 77).
2. No question, no number, no side taken, no other truck, no other person.
3. One a week.
4. The first hit arrives when the caption finally says something ("That is sOoOo tAcKeY", "Glow plugs it is", "Haters always talking") and the view floor moves the same week.

**Build episodes inside the first 30**: nobody's first 30 posts contain a build update that outperformed the account's median. Dentside Depot's first four long videos are a four-part build (3.6K, 5K, 2.4K, 18K); his first four Shorts over 100K are a warning, a fix, an ID tip and an opinion. Travis's first five videos are a washing machine; the formula only clicks on video 6 when the title asks a question with a number in it. Smiths' first above-median post (post 4, 20K) is a solution to a known 6.0 problem, not a shop tour.

**What the first 30 were NOT**: nobody opened with a lockup, a channel trailer (DriveHub's "This Is DriveNH" trailer did 1.2K), an introduction, a thank-you, or a partner.

## 5. The common breakout mechanism

Across 12 accounts and 14,000 posts, the post that moved the floor was a reaction to something outside the creator's own garage, with the creator's truck or trade as the authority behind the reaction. The sub-types, with the cleanest example of each:

1. **Rate or roast a submitted truck** (Robert post 70, 377K; Zac's "What wheels should I do next" 589K; DDC's "Rating our followers builds" is the failed club version at 3K). Why it works: the owner shares it, the owner's friends argue in the comments, and the next 50 posts are already supplied.
2. **Reply to a hater with the truck as the punchline** (Konner 305K, Tyler 300K, Blake's "Be a man" 206K, Diesel Queen's "#genzsucks" 415K at post 28). Why: a side was taken in the first second and the comment section becomes the content.
3. **Stitch or duet a claim** (gearman 360K at post 15, Robert's duet 1.0M, Diesel Queen's 24M, Cowgirl's "Reply to @" posts at 3.6M to 8.7M). Why: the other creator's audience is delivered to you.
4. **A take about the platform, not the project** (Dentside Depot "never buy a painted classic Ford" 254K at Short 8, "classic truck better than an EV" 3.5M; Zac "GM is bringing back the square body" 12.6M; Robert "5th gen cummins arent real" 1.1M). Why: everyone who owns one has an opinion and shares to prove it.
5. **A customer or stranger story with a number** (Smiths' warranty story 362K; Caylee's "$2,000 truck, $2,500 in parts, 60 hours" 1.4M; Travis "2,018 miles home" 1.55M; Jeff "only had it 15 days" 141K, "2 trucks for $500" 23K at post 18).
6. **The thing failing** (DriveHub "Rare 7.3 Won't Start" 5.76M against a 279K finale; Travis's "will it run" titles; Caylee's "My engines keep breaking and I don't know why" 113K).

What did NOT break anyone out: a build update, a reveal, a partner thank-you, a shop tour, a channel trailer, a milestone thank-you (Robert's "45k on Instagram" did 20K against a 377K roast the same week; Zac's year-end thank-you did 47K against a 605K rating).

What the build itself does: it is the reason people stay, and it pays once per project at episode 1 (DriveHub 197K, Travis 235K, both the top of their series) and sometimes once at the end (DriveHub 279K; Travis and Smiths got no finale pop). The twelve episodes in the middle ran at 25 to 50 percent of episode 1 for both long-form channels. Plan for that: the middle of Rival will not grow the account; the reactions between episodes will.

What happened after: every account that moved to a sponsor-tag feed (Diesel Queen, Cowgirl, gearman's tool posts, Chris's TikTok Shop posts) kept the view spikes on paid posts and lost the like rate and the organic median. The accounts whose median is still rising (Blake, Smiths in 2026Q3) show the sponsor inside a customer job or an event rather than as the post.

## 6. What the set does that @elcoyotediesel does not (aggregated)

1. Puts other people's trucks on the page and reacts to them. Jacob's grid is his own truck, his own memes and his own partners.
2. Writes a sentence in line 1 that takes a side, asks a question or states a number. The account's build captions are descriptions.
3. Posted 3 to 6 a day for the first month (the fast group). Jacob is at about 1.5 a day with the build tier eating the time.
4. Lets the truck break on camera and posts the break as its own clip within 48 hours (DriveHub's 5.76M).
5. Keeps one length (Zac 60 to 80 s, Robert 6 to 30 s, Dentside 25 s) for years.
6. Dates its own milestones in a caption once or twice a year (Zac, Robert, Blake), which is also how a stranger learns the account is moving.
7. Shows the partner's part going in on a customer truck or at an event, never as a standalone thank-you (Blake, Smiths, Dentside Depot).
8. Uses the trade as authority in a reaction, not as a story: gearman shows the inside of the transmission while disagreeing with someone; Chris's first hit is a two-word diagnosis.
