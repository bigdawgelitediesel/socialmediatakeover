## 1. Your Posting Cadence [1:23:56 to 2:08:12]

### 1a. Quality versus quantity, and the accordion method [1:24 to 1:27]

- [1:24] He says he got the cadence question "at least 300 times in the last year". People argue quality versus quantity without defining either.
- [1:24 to 1:25] Common view of quantity: post a lot to get the most impressions and "show up everywhere". He calls that "a beautiful byproduct", not the purpose.
- [1:25] Named framework: "the accordion method". Volume is a tool inside it.
- [1:25] Rule: "I don't know what quality is and neither do you." Calling a piece high quality is a subjective opinion. "Your audience is who determines what quality is, not you. And we use volume to acquire that data quicker."
- [1:25] Number example: 10 videos over 30 days is one every 3 days. The same 10 videos posted in one week gives the insights faster.
- [1:26] The insights: what the audience resonates with, likes more, comments on more, what gets more views. Those are the audience "signaling" what is higher quality.
- [1:26] Quality has nothing to do with lights, camera or set. "Some of the most viral videos... are filmed at night in the dark on a cell phone."
- [1:26 to 1:27] The method in two moves: (1) post high volume to learn what the audience says is quality, (2) "you compress the accordion and that's when you start to put more effort per piece of content."
- [1:27] GARBLED: "I don't want to spoil the mana right now" (meaning unclear, he is saying more on this comes later).

### 1b. Framework for determining your posting cadence [1:27 to 1:36]

Four points, in his order:

1. **Start with the highest leverage platform** [1:27 to 1:29]
   - Two tests. One: where do you get the most results (leads, most engagement, what performs well "by the platform standards and by conversion standards"). Two: which platform lets you make one piece that can be repurposed into many.
   - [1:27 to 1:28] Typical answer is long form (a YouTube video): clip moments for shorts, pull the transcript for quotes, use the transcript for a LinkedIn post, extract the audio for a podcast, pull stills for social posts.
   - [1:28] Rule: if you track off-platform conversions ("which you should") and one or two platforms lead to far more conversions, "double, triple, quadruple down on those platforms."
   - [1:28 to 1:29] Example: on one team YouTube was the number one lead provider, Instagram two, LinkedIn three, by absolute count. By percentage of audience converting, LinkedIn was "number one by a mile" on a much smaller audience. They moved far more resources to LinkedIn and saw "massive returns"; they were spending "probably 20 $30,000 a month just on LinkedIn organic content alone". Lesson he shouts: "track your data."

2. **Volume for speed and learnings, not just reach** [1:30 to 1:32]
   - [1:30] "In the early days... whatever volume you think you need to do right now, increase it." You want to know what the audience deems quality as soon as possible.
   - [1:30] Example: a previous team started YouTube at three videos a week, then five. "I wouldn't even call them edited. They were just trimmed." No graphics, no crafted intro or packaging. "We were at the learning acquisition phase."
   - [1:31] Then do more of what they resonate with, and "more" means "putting more effort per piece", not more volume. Example: take the effort of five videos a week and put it into one, built on what the audience told you.
   - [1:31] Rule: "Quality is not your personal preference. Quality is determined by your audience."
   - [1:31 to 1:32] Trap: "you are making the decision for your audience", sitting on a piece because you think it is not good enough. "How do you know if you don't put it out?" Mature creators can tell a piece will miss; early on "you don't have a clue." "Stop guessing. Stop making the decision for them."

3. **Avoid the perfect post trap** [1:32 to 1:34]
   - [1:32] Rule: "perfect is the enemy of posted."
   - [1:32 to 1:33] Daily posting is "an absurd level to just immediately jump into". Consider three times a week, once a week, once every other week.
   - [1:33] His own cadence: about one long form YouTube video a month, "maybe every three weeks or so", more effort per piece.
   - [1:33 to 1:34] Why he skips the high-volume phase: in the last year he met with over 2,000 businesses and ran Q&A sessions, so he already knows the common questions and what people need.
   - [1:34] Rule: unless you have that kind of insight into your ideal customer, start with high volume. But "if high volume means one post every other week in the beginning because that's all you can do, then do that. Whatever you can actually put out consistently, please start with that."
   - [1:34] Analogy: "Don't try and jump to the Ronnie Coleman method and squat 800 lb when you've never squatted 135."

4. **Stop aiming for perfection, speed wins** [1:34 to 1:36]
   - [1:34 to 1:35] Example: while filming this course, semis and police sirens go by. They do not stop or change location. They post it anyway, and every night after filming they work out what they would do differently next time.
   - [1:35 to 1:36] Rule: do not use the problems you see in a piece as a reason not to post. "Post it and take note of what you're going to do on future pieces that you make." (GARBLED: captions say "Perfect is the enemy of Postit", he means posted.)

### 1c. Framework for increasing your volume [1:36 to 1:48]

- [1:36] If you have posted consistently for 2 or 3 months "you are in the top 1%"; many give up after a week or two.

1. **Leverage a content pillar approach** [1:36 to 1:41]
   - [1:36] Identify your pillar content, usually long form: a YouTube video, a deep-dive thread, a long written LinkedIn post, a newsletter.
   - [1:36] Named step: "mining". "Go through your long-form content and mine for the gold."
   - [1:37] Mining a newsletter gives three things: (a) "bite-sized chunks that are self-contained", a paragraph that makes sense and has value without the rest, (b) a quote or two, (c) the whole newsletter summarized in five or six sentences as a medium-form post.
   - [1:37] Mining a YouTube video: find the moments that will perform. Look for "a powerful moment that maybe is a slightly contrarian view or is a nuanced take on an already discussed subject." His example of such a moment: his own quality versus quantity passage.
   - [1:38] Named framework: "the waterfall distribution model". He credits Gary Vee (Gary Vaynerchuk) with the idea, in use since about 2015 or 2016 (DailyVee clips posted to Instagram before Reels and TikTok).
   - [1:38 to 1:39] The waterfall from one YouTube video, in his list:
     1. Short form clips.
     2. Quotes for Threads and X.
     3. A transcript moment rewritten as a LinkedIn post.
     4. That LinkedIn post also posted on Facebook.
     5. One of the shorts posted on Instagram with the LinkedIn post as its caption.
     6. Carousels: two carousel slides per section of the video.
   - [1:39] Carousel rules: simplify, no heavy text. "One very basic, very easy to understand image followed by one, two sentences at most per slide."
   - [1:39 to 1:40] Carousel hook rule: the first two slides are taken from the hook made for the YouTube video. Reason: if a follower did not engage the first time, Instagram serves the carousel again starting on slide two, "a second at-bat". "You want to ensure that you have a hook for one and two."
   - [1:40] Observation, "not a rule", as of February 2025: listicles do better in carousel form. Example title: "10 things I learned about investing in my 30s".
   - [1:40 to 1:41] A complex deep dive should not be compressed into a carousel. Take one section of it and expand that into the carousel.
   - [1:41] Result: "easily over 10 pieces of content" from one filmed video. "Very high leverage activity."

2. **Scale by platform, not all at once** [1:41 to 1:45]
   - [1:41] Order so far: pick medium, pick platforms, set cadence, post consistently on two or three priority platforms.
   - [1:41] Uses his earlier named approach, the "Eye of Sauron approach" (GARBLED: captions say "I of Sauron"; it was introduced before this range). Rule: "Do not try to scale up all three or two platforms that you are emphasizing at once. If you do this, you won't do any of them well."
   - [1:41 to 1:42] Example ranking: LinkedIn, YouTube, Instagram. Attack volume on number one first. Put the others on "maintenance mode": enough content to keep the current audience engaged, not trying to grow.
   - [1:42] Two ways to scale the focus platform: increase the number of posts, or "increase the amount of effort you put per post." As of February 2025 he would start with more effort per post. He says this is "way different than what I would have said a year ago."
   - [1:42 to 1:43] Example: posting five times a week on LinkedIn, put that effort into three. "You get far more out of a very, very extensive and high value piece of content than you do off of medium value content at a high volume."
   - [1:43 to 1:44] Example: a client wanted at least four YouTube videos a month; they averaged about 250,000 views each (a million a month). He argues one video a month with a million views is worth more. "It's the same number of views. It's not the same number of eyeballs." The million-view video had a high share of new audience. On the 250,000 videos, "more than 60 to 70% of the views were from the current audience."
   - [1:44 to 1:45] Example: MrBeast (Jimmy Donaldson) posted a video every 4 to 8 weeks, sometimes two months apart, putting all the effort into each one so more people saw and shared it.
   - [1:45] He says "lower volume, higher effort per piece", not "higher quality", because quality is subjective. "I don't want to... make you think that I know what quality is. I don't. You do."

3. **Study your top 10%** [1:45 to 1:48]
   - [1:45 to 1:46] After a period of volume you know which topics and structures or formats work. Do less of the rest and more of that.
   - [1:46] Named hack: "Look at the top 10% performing content." Take the last 90 days (or 30 if that is all you have, "whatever amount of time that you can do up to 90 days").
   - [1:46] What to look for: not every possible reason a post did well, but "what occurred in all of these pieces of content that I can then replicate and put into every piece I do moving forward." If it shows up in only one or two, "it's kind of a crapshoot." Usually you find one, maybe two things.
   - [1:47] Rule: learn from top content, not general content. "Why would you want to learn from the bottom 10% or the bottom 20%."
   - [1:47] Increase volume on a platform because it drives more conversions, whatever conversion means for you (newsletter signups, music streams, film downloads). "Optimize for conversions first."
   - [1:47 to 1:48] If content is not performing well: do the same thing. Some content still does better than the rest, so study that top 10%.
   - [1:48] Cadence of the review: monthly. Re-evaluating each month means you keep improving off your best work instead of sticking with findings from 6 months ago. (GARBLED: "always going to be lading up", likely "laddering up".)

### 1d. Make your content sustainable [1:48 to 2:07]

Six points, in his order:

1. **Play for the long term** [1:48 to 1:49]
   - "If you hate video, you're not going to stick with it. So, don't do video." Start with audio, design or written; comfort with video may come later.
   - Rule: "Content creation should be sustainable for years, not months."

2. **Build a simple content machine** [1:49 to 1:50]
   - "We're not Mr. Beast on day one." Set a baseline. Example baseline: one YouTube video every two weeks plus three shorts a week on Instagram and YouTube Shorts.
   - Hold the baseline for 2 or 3 months, then raise the bar.
   - Batching: some people block a 12-hour day and film three or four YouTube videos, some shorts and a couple of podcasts. His observation ("not a rule"): for many people that puts so much pressure on the day that they perform worse, get anxious or get sick. A low-pressure pattern of a video here and a video there can work better.
   - Rule: "make sure you are aligning your strategy with your calendar and your schedule." Ask what time you really have: through the week, or batch only.

3. **Remove yourself from the execution** (team) [1:51 to 1:59]
   - [1:51] If you can afford it, build a team. Start with freelancers or agencies to keep upfront cost low and test for a positive return before reinvesting.
   - [1:52] Why filming is hard for the person on camera. During the session: Is this interesting? Is it valuable? Will people learn and change what they do? Are they enjoying this? Is this boring? After: the "inner critic monologue" on the drive home, "I could have done so much better."
   - [1:53] Rule: "it is not the content creator's job to create the right environment. It's the team's job." He has a separate downloadable playbook on making talent comfortable.
   - Tactics he gives (all come from the principle "making a conducive environment to capture great content" [1:55]):
     1. [1:53 to 1:55] The videographer gives visual feedback while filming: nods, with more intensity as the point gets better, and thumbs up. It is real-time feedback because the real feedback loop is long (this course would not come out for at least 6 weeks, more likely two months). The talent learns to go deeper on a good moment and knows they are not boring.
     2. [1:55 to 1:56] At every break, a fist pound or high five, "that was great", plus one or two specific things that stood out. It keeps momentum and gives energy. The person behind the camera raises their own energy when the talent fades, and the talent matches it.
     3. [1:56 to 1:57] The "2.0 version": the team also thinks "through the frame of a skeptic", listing the objections the audience will have, the way a good video sales letter overcomes sales objections before the call. After each section he and Trevor ask what holes could be poked, what context is missing, what questions can be answered before they are asked. Doing this builds more trust. (GARBLED: captions say "VSSL", he means VSL.)
     4. [1:57 to 1:59] 15 to 20 minutes after the session, send the talent a message saying how well they did, naming one or two things they said (the content, not the lighting). If the session was rough, do not lie: "I am so impressed with how you showed up and still pushed through despite this, this, and this." Rule: never give false praise, because they stop trusting your compliments.
   - [1:59] Why it works, in his branding terms: it pairs film days with good feelings so the talent looks forward to them instead of dreading them.

4. **Systematize repurposing, and make content contextual to the platform** [1:59 to 2:05]
   - [1:59 to 2:00] Qualifier: the waterfall method is "a great way to start", but in 2025 the platforms are fragmented and behave differently. "The best version of content... is making content contextual to the platform itself", native to TikTok, Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube long form.
   - [2:00] "More podcast clips don't do well than podcast clips that do." For predictive power, make it contextual. Early on ("first 3 years even") you will mostly be repurposing.
   - [2:00 to 2:01] The "2.0 version" of repurposing: "using insights of how content has performed on one platform in one medium and using that to inform new mediums for new platforms."
   - [2:01] Named practice: "social listening". Paying attention to what the audience is saying is quality.
   - [2:01 to 2:03] Full example, a client's one-line statement (he cannot recall the wording):
     1. Posted as a tweet. One of her most liked tweets, many replies, many people sharing it to their stories and tagging her.
     2. A short: the tweet used word for word as the hook, then she explains why she said it, the story and the situation behind it.
     3. A printed poster of the quote, hung in a good-looking setting and photographed. One of, if not her top Instagram post of all time. Tactic he notes ("a tactic, not a principle"): photos of a real-life thing, such as writing on a post-it note or a printed quote, do well on social right now.
     4. A longer LinkedIn post with the quote as the hook, kept unchanged because it was already proven, with her short-form commentary expanded. Top three, if not her top LinkedIn post.
     5. A 25 to 30 minute YouTube video on the quote, with a version of the quote as the title. In her top 10 videos.
   - [2:04] Rule: they did not repost the tweet everywhere. "Make the content contextual to the platform. Reverse engineer what the platform is telling you it wants and what its users want and make it in that way. It can be the same exact message but repackaged differently."
   - [2:04 to 2:05] Analogy, the "Sarah" analogy: Sarah goes to family brunch on Saturday, cocktails with friends Saturday night, a business meeting Monday. She is Sarah at all three but talks about different topics, in a different tone, with different vocabulary. "That's not her being inauthentic. That is her revealing different parts of herself contextual to the environment she is in." Platforms are "just these different events that you're going to."

5. **Define your energy rhythm** [2:05 to 2:06]
   - "Knowing thyself": know when you have the most energy and clearest thinking, and schedule content creation there. His own pattern: best in the morning, tired around midday, picks back up late in the day.
   - Filming at your worst time means you enjoy it less and it performs worse.

6. **Protect your content time like an important business meeting** (sub-point) [2:06 to 2:07]
   - People keep moving their content day. A real priority (a buyer meeting) can outrank it, but if you keep rescheduling, an immature team reads content as not a priority for you and it becomes less of a priority for them.

## 2. Storytelling In Your Content [2:08:12 to 2:34:17]

### 2a. Why story [2:08 to 2:09]

- [2:08] Even MrBeast, known more for "adrenaline", is using more storytelling tactics, visible in Beast Games.
- [2:08 to 2:09] What stories do, his list:
  1. Entertain and engage.
  2. Change people's perspective: let them see a circumstance they have been dealing with from a new angle.
  3. Keep people hooked, retain attention ("attention is the greatest asset right now").
  4. Create an emotional connection between you and the audience.
  5. Give depth and round out the concept or principle you are sharing.
  6. Get remembered: "People have a harder time remembering facts, but what they do remember is the stories that you tell them and the new perspective they were able to gain."

### 2b. The storytelling framework: hook, problem, journey, lesson, call to action [2:09 to 2:19]

- [2:09] The five parts, in order: "the hook, the problem, the journey, the lesson, and then the call to action."
- [2:09] Rule on how to use it: "do not operate off of this framework like it's something rigid... You now have the rules. Feel free to break the rules." Move parts around, use it "in a fluid way rather than a rigid way."

**Part 1. The hook** [2:09 to 2:12]

- [2:09 to 2:10] "We have to get attention fast." You compete with every other creator, with their friends, and with the dog barking at them.
- [2:10] Timing rule for short form (Instagram, TikTok, Facebook Reels, YouTube Shorts, LinkedIn video tab): "you have about two or three seconds to get their attention." On some platforms "only like a second or two." Miss it and they have "already scrolled five posts further down."
- [2:10] Timing for YouTube long form: the industry says 30 seconds. He disagrees: "about 15 to 20 seconds max."
- [2:10 to 2:11] What the long form viewer checks in that window: (1) is this video what I clicked on (does it match the packaging), or am I being "Rick rolled", (2) "is this the person that I want to listen to telling me this information?"
- [2:11] Hook types he names:
  1. A "very bold statement".
  2. A "contrarian view", something you see differently from your counterparts or the experts in your field.
  3. An "unexpected fact", though he says these "are kind of getting a little tiresome at this point."
  4. A "pattern interrupt" [2:12].
  5. A "thought-provoking question" [2:12].
- [2:11] Rule: "if you say something that most people believe to be true and you claim it to be false and give an alternative take, you're almost always going to catch people's attention."
- [2:11 to 2:12] Example 1, contrarian statement: "Subscribers, views, likes don't matter. What actually matters is..." It works because most content experts say the opposite, so "it stands out."
- [2:12] Example 2, pattern interrupt: the "blueberry clip", one of the top clips he made for Gary Vaynerchuk and Gary's most viral clip at the time. Gary is chomping blueberries and opens with "I eat blueberries like it's my job." Next line: "You missed the first 30 I ate."
- [2:12] Goal of all of them: "a thumb stopper", something that makes them need to watch the rest.

**Part 2. The problem** [2:12 to 2:13]

- [2:12] "This is where you're going to set up the stakes." Introduce "a conflict or some sort of emotional investment."
- [2:13] It does not need to be big or dramatic. You need "some sort of problem or thing that the main character has to overcome."
- [2:13] Rule: "make the problem specific and relatable." The audience should think "I've gone through something similar" and see themselves in it.
- [2:13] Example line: "I was working 14 hours a day for my hero, Gary Vaynerchuk, but I still felt stuck." It creates stakes: it shows how much he cared and the inner turmoil.

**Part 3. The journey** [2:13 to 2:16]

- [2:13] "This is how we overcome it." In classic terms, "how the prince slays the dragon to rescue the princess."
- [2:13 to 2:14] Rule: "people resonate with progress, not perfection." Do not share only highlights. Share the low points and the mistakes.
- [2:14] For his example he would tell how he got unstuck "through a sequence of victories and losses."
- [2:14] Rule: people "relate to your struggles, I would argue, more than your successes."
- [2:14] Named idea: "show the messy middle." Show where you doubt yourself or the opportunity.
- [2:14 to 2:15] Examples of the messy middle:
  - A business owner unsure it will work, whether they can provide for their family, or whether they will have to go back to a 9 to 5.
  - A rock musician trying country, unsure that audience will take it. The audience connects more when you explain why you are trying the new thing and admit you do not know how it will go.
  - Himself: he has said through the course that he thinks this project will matter and help his consulting firm, but he does not know. He is spending a lot of money and three days of filming on it. (GARBLED: firm name captioned "Rston", Ralston.)
  - [2:16] The fifth grade science volcano: you put a lot in and did not know if it would erupt for the teacher. (Teacher's name captioned "Mrs. Fields", unverified.)
- [2:16] He calls this the key: "in your storytelling framework this is the key. We want to share the transparency... We want the audience to see themself through you in some way."
- [2:16] Example line: "I realized I was burning out because I was trying to do everything and that's when I realized that I could do [blank]." It gives a look at the journey and names a mistake.

**Part 4. The lesson** [2:16 to 2:18]

- [2:16] "This is where you make it useful for your audience." Every story should give a new perspective, a shift.
- [2:17] Give "the lesson, the takeaway, the thing that they can act on. Otherwise... they're being entertained", and they just say "cool, great story."
- [2:17] Make acting easy: "step-by-step instructions, chunking it down."
- [2:17] Definition of value: "providing value is something that is useful, something that the audience can take and immediately put into action."
- [2:17] Example transition line: "Here's what I learned from that season of my career and here are three ways that you can apply these learnings in your career so that you avoid the mistakes that I made."
- [2:17 to 2:18] Tie the lesson back to your expertise and, ideally, to your offer. "Every time that we can tie our lessons, our stories back to that, the better."

**Part 5. The CTA (call to action)** [2:18 to 2:19]

- [2:18] Most people hear CTA and think "go to my website and buy my offer". He says that is not required. "A call to action just literally means telling people to take action. It doesn't mean that you have to sell them something."
- [2:18] Rule: "your best content is not going to be pushing and selling." The later-stage version with a budget is to retarget those viewers with ads. (GARBLED: captions say "retarget those individuals who paid"; in context he means retargeting viewers through paid ads. Unsure.)
- [2:18 to 2:19] Simple CTA example: "comment below on what your biggest takeaway was and how you're going to apply it to your life moving forward."
- [2:19] Why that works: it drives engagement, makes the viewer name what they will do, and raises the chance they change their actions.
- [2:19] Full example: "If you're struggling with trying to do everything yourself and burning out, try delegating and training your team... comment below, tell me which of these tactics you're going to use."
- [2:19] What the CTA is for: it "bridges the gap between engagement and viewership and actually taking action. And that's the ultimate goal of educational content."

**The five example lines strung together (his running example)**
1. Hook: a contrarian statement, such as "Subscribers, views, likes don't matter. What actually matters is..."
2. Problem: "I was working 14 hours a day for my hero, Gary Vaynerchuk, but I still felt stuck."
3. Journey: "I realized I was burning out because I was trying to do everything and that's when I realized that I could do [blank]."
4. Lesson: "Here's what I learned from that season of my career and here are three ways that you can apply these learnings..."
5. CTA: "Comment below, tell me which of these tactics you're going to use..."

### 2c. The best types of stories to tell [2:20 to 2:29]

- [2:20] "Not all stories work equally as well in content." Five categories:

**1. Origin stories** [2:20 to 2:23]
- [2:20] After years you will be bored of your origin story and want your editors to cut it. "Do not do that."
- [2:20] Rule: "You need to treat your content like you're always trying to reach a cold audience because that's what you're trying to do." A cold audience does not know your origin story.
- [2:20] He says origin stories are what people resonate with "the most". It is how you got to where you are.
- [2:20 to 2:21] Rule: "The thing that's not relatable is where you're at right now." Example: a businesswoman with a $500 million net worth on the cover of Forbes is not relatable. How she went from a Starbucks barista barely making ends meet to that is.
- [2:21] What an origin story does: tells the audience why you do what you do (Peter Parker bitten by the spider, Batman and his parents), and shows the pivotal moments that shaped your career and you. Without it they will not understand why you say and do things the way you do.
- [2:21 to 2:22] His commitment: keep going deeper into his own origin story over the next year.
- [2:22 to 2:23] His examples: things in his life before working on set gave him his approach to making talent comfortable. In his first YouTube video (his career journey) he tells how at 15 someone gave him Gary Vaynerchuk's book Crush It, which made him see he could do video content and explains why he bet on social media when other marketers called it a fad, and why he references Gary so often. Without that story the constant Gary references would look odd. (GARBLED: the name of the person who gave him the book is captioned "Sean Kel"; spelling unverified.)

**2. Failure stories** [2:23 to 2:24]
- [2:23] "Most humans have one thing in common, which is we fail." Failure stories make you relatable and build trust.
- [2:23] Rule: "People trust vulnerability more than they trust success. They trust failure more than they trust wins."
- [2:23] Rule: "don't only talk about what went wrong. Don't make it just a sob story." Share the lessons so others can avoid the same loss of time or money.
- [2:24] Example from his career video: he signed two legal cannabis companies in Washington state, tripled his income, felt like a big shot. Then that same client cut his monthly pay in half and he had to fire three friends he had hired. He told the story, what he learned, how he will never repeat it, and spelled out how the viewer can avoid the same mistake. (GARBLED: "I502" is how the captions give the cannabis companies' label; and he says "two... companies" then "that same client", so the count is unclear.)

**3. Success stories** [2:25 to 2:26]
- [2:25] As important as failures. Without them "people will just think that you are a failure constantly learning." You want the audience to associate you with success.
- [2:25] Show "before and after transformations."
- [2:25] Success stories are the proof that you are the right person to listen to on the subject, which raises trust.
- [2:25] "Highlight small wins that became big results". This builds credibility.
- [2:25 to 2:26] His example, which he says he references as often as possible: he worked with Gary to grow his TikTok "from 300,000 followers to 3.5 million in just 3 months."

**4. Customer and client success** [2:26 to 2:28]
- [2:26] Proof that you can do it for others, that the success can be replicated. The audience gets to see themselves in the story and then go take action.
- [2:26 to 2:28] Full example: about 2 years ago an agency owner asked how to get more out of his editors. Ralston gave one tactic from the principle "let them know what you like so they do more of it": instead of only leaving review comments on what to change, spend at least the same time on comments that reinforce what to keep. **5. Industry stories** [2:28 to 2:29]
- [2:28] He takes a contrarian view here. Do not be another person agreeing with everyone and repeating the same things. Agreeing content is fine to make but is less good at catching attention and starting conversation.
- [2:28] Method: look at what people in your industry are saying. Where you differ, say so, respectfully, without naming names. "Share the industry trope that everybody touts as gospel. And then share your contrary belief."
- [2:28 to 2:29] Example: many people tell creators to make heavy calls to action for their offer in organic content. He believes the opposite, so the video opens: "Never tell your audience about your offer in your organic content." Then expand. It flips a common trope, catches attention and gives a view most have not heard.

### 2d. The purpose of educational content [2:29 to 2:34]

- [2:29] For people making entertainment this "might not be as useful". For educational creators he calls it "possibly the most critical next few minutes of this entire course."
- [2:29 to 2:30] His definitions, restated: "Branding is the pairing of things. And good branding is an intentional pairing of relevant things consistently which then creates the byproduct which is brand which is when the audience inherently associates the two things."
- [2:30 to 2:31] The chain, step by step:
  1. Share success stories. You are pairing yourself with success stories.
  2. Over time the audience associates you with success.
  3. That causes trust in what you say.
  4. Because they trust you, they hear the lessons and takeaways.
  5. Because they trust the lessons, they take action. "This is when the gold starts."
  6. They get the results they wanted, the success they heard about in your client stories, for themselves.
  7. When that happens consistently, "the audience doesn't associate you with success stories. The audience begins to associate you with their success."
- [2:31 to 2:32] Why that matters: they learn, consciously or not, "that they always get more than what they invest with you", because all they invested was time to watch and time to act (sometimes money, such as for paid ads, but the outcome is still bigger).
- [2:32] So when you finally tell them about your offer, they trust that what they put in is small next to what they will get.
- [2:32 to 2:33] Example: a company putting out a lot of long form educational content on LinkedIn and YouTube started in-person seminars. He asked attendees what the sales call was like. Nearly all said the same: they told the salesperson to stop talking and take their credit card, because the free content had shown them the paid thing would be worth far more than the price. Paying $1,000 for the seminar, they expected at least $10,000 in value; he says they were getting "50 to 100x".
- [2:33 to 2:34] Rule, stated twice: "educational content is just scaling trust." And "trust makes everything in business and life easier." (GARBLED: "doors... fleeing wide open", he means fling.)

## 3. Community Driven Content [2:34:17 to 2:45:24]

### 3a. Involve your audience in what you create [2:34 to 2:36]

- [2:34 to 2:35] Example from the night before: a musician at the studio. Musicians often have several versions of a verse or a guitar lick. Post the two options and let the audience say which they like more. They become invested in the release.
- [2:35] Same for authors: two versions of a chapter, two versions of the book title. He says it applies to any medium.
- [2:35 to 2:36] Two payoffs: (1) you make something you know they want because they told you, (2) they are bought in, they become "your advocates... your missionaries", telling others because they were part of the process.
- [2:36] Named mindset shift: "shifting from creator first to community first." "It's no longer just a one-way talking at them. It's talking with them."

### 3b. Create feedback loops: listen and adapt [2:36 to 2:38]

- [2:36] "Listen and adapt. Listen and iterate." Rule: "the best content ideas actually come directly from your audience."
- [2:36 to 2:37] He does not mean a poll asking "What do you want to hear from me?" He likes that for Q&A formats only. "They don't know what they want... they don't know what they need." A "what should I make next" poll is "way too blue ocean" with no constraints to choose within.
- [2:37] Where the insight is: "the questions in your DMs, the questions in your comment section."
- [2:37 to 2:38] Example, Gary Vaynerchuk and "social listening": he reads tweet replies, Instagram DMs and comments. When, say, five people in a day ask the same or a similar question, he makes content answering it right away.
- [2:38] Rule in the example: he does not open with "so many people have been asking me about [blank]." "He immediately jumps into it and addresses the problem or question."
- [2:38] Result: comments like "How did you know I needed this right now?" The reasoning: if five people asked in the last 3 hours, thousands are going through the same thing right now. Content made this way is "literally speaking to them in their exact moment right now, aka reading their mind."

### 3c. Make your audience the hero of the story [2:38 to 2:40]

- [2:38 to 2:39] It makes them want to act, get the result and become one of the success stories. In practice: "sharing community wins, transformations, and case studies."
- [2:39] Example: a free weekly Zoom call where someone acts on what you shared and it changes their life. Share that on social, not only inside the community. Two effects: (1) current members are moved to act and win so they can be a story, (2) skeptical outsiders trust you more.
- [2:39 to 2:40] Example: someone DMs that they applied your lesson and got the result. Turn it into content, for instance record an Instagram video call with them and post it.
- [2:40] Honest caveat: "this is not going to be your top performing content by any means." Not many views or likes. What it does is encourage future people to engage and send you their own wins.

### 3d. Leverage user generated content [2:40 to 2:41]

- [2:40] (GARBLED: captions say "UC", he means UGC.) "I would encourage your audience to create with you."
- [2:40 to 2:41] Method: put out a prompt asking the audience to share an insight or story based on your lessons. Take the videos they post (TikTok, Reels) and stitch them, building content on top of theirs.
- [2:41] Result: "a perpetual cycle of engagement." It moves the audience to create with you and to promote you to their own audience.
- [2:41] Rule: "no audience is too small. Like even people that have 300 followers, I want them informing their 300 followers about my brand."

### 3e. The three pillars of community content [2:41 to 2:42]

1. **Educational content**: teaching the community. "We help the community by solving their biggest problems."
2. **Conversational content**: the purpose is to engage. "Make your audience feel heard and involved in your content." Two-way, not one-way.
3. **Social proof content**: showcase community wins; highlight real stories from the community and the impact your content has there.

### 3f. How to turn viewers into community members [2:42 to 2:45]

1. **Make engagement the goal, not just views** [2:42 to 2:43]
   - Create content that invites them to take part.
   - Simple version: ask them to "drop a fire emoji in the comments if they found this clip useful."
   - Stronger version: ask them to debate in the comments. Share a take that runs against the industry norm ("not controversy for controversial sake") and make the CTA "debate whether or not you agree with this in the comments below."
   - Strongest version: "I would love to know your two cents on this. I'm going to be engaging with some of you and going back and forth."

2. **Respond and reward participation** [2:43 to 2:44]
   - The number one thing he sees in people who ask how to get more engagement: "the three people that are currently commenting on their content, they're not responding to."
   - Rule: "If they see that you are more likely to respond to comments, they are more likely to comment." You are setting an example for the next new person. "People don't like one-way conversations."
   - Replying thanks the people who engage and signals to the rest that they can go from viewer to community member.

3. **Create inside jokes and shared language** [2:44 to 2:45]
   - "Really strong communities have very unique terminology and shared language that they all understand and outsiders do not."
   - Examples: gym bros have "bulking season"; "the Harley community has all their jokes about sportster riders."
   - Two ways to do it: (a) use an inside joke or lingo that already exists in your niche, which shows you are an insider who speaks their language, or (b) create your own sayings.
   - Question he poses: "when you're thinking about your community and your niche, what's the inside joke?"

## 4. Scale Your Content [2:45:24 to 3:12:56]

- [2:45] "More content equals more opportunities to engage, more opportunities to attract, and more opportunities to convert." But scaling must not mean burning out or quitting early. Build a process that is sustainable.

### 4a. Platform specific content [2:46 to 2:48]

- [2:46] The key is the earlier analogy again: you are you everywhere, but you show a different side per setting. His version: riding with the boys versus breakfast with his mom. "You're going to talk way differently on LinkedIn than you are on TikTok."
- [2:47] Up to now you made one piece and posted the clips everywhere. That was a great start and it hits a ceiling.
- [2:47] (team) To break the ceiling: the same source media goes to maybe five different editors who each cut it for their own platform. It "looks massively inefficient."
- [2:47 to 2:48] Rule about hooks: "the meat of the video stays roughly the same, but the entry point into the meat of the video, the hook might be different because people are looking for different things or solving different problems on LinkedIn versus TikTok." Three platforms "might require a completely different hook."
- [2:48] Rule: "the way you got to one point is not the way you're going to get to the next point."

### 4b. Assign platforms to creators, the 85/15 split (team) [2:48 to 2:52]

- [2:48] Match each creator's skills and interests to a platform so they are "living, eating, and breathing this platform."
- [2:48 to 2:49] Example: when Gary gave him TikTok at 300,000 followers, he made no content the first week. He scrolled TikTok eight hours a day for a week, taking notes on what worked and pulling out what could apply to Gary's content.
- [2:49] The pairing: rank six platforms (top three priority, bottom three where you only redistribute). Best editor gets platform 1 and platform 4. Second best gets 2 and 5. Third gets 3 and 6.
- [2:49 to 2:50] Named split: "85%" of their time on platform-native content for their top platform, "the remaining 15%" experimenting with native content for their second platform. Reason: the top three platforms drive the economics.
- [2:50 to 2:51] Example: Angela, the best editor, owns Instagram (85%) and TikTok (15%). That looks like about seven original pieces a week for Instagram and one or two for TikTok. Her Instagram pieces can still be redistributed elsewhere, but she thinks only about the Instagram audience.
- [2:51] This is the Eye of Sauron approach handed down to each team member: prioritize the platform that drives results, test on the second, because platforms change and a secondary one can jump in priority.
- [2:51 to 2:52] Result on one of his teams, already getting billions of impressions a year: "a 1.5x return." Analogy: a new lifter doubles lifts every couple of months; at a 600 or 700 lb squat a 5% gain can be the gap between a standard lift and a world record.

### 4c. Why it works [2:52 to 2:56]

1. [2:52 to 2:53] **More focus.** No more "vanilla content", made hoping it works everywhere.
2. [2:53] **Creators are tied to performance.** A shorts editor cannot track one clip across five to ten platforms. Someone who owns one channel can, and is more accountable. He calls it "the beautiful marriage of creative and data."
3. [2:53 to 2:54] **Data and creative sit close together.** The traditional agency model puts data and strategy in a different house from creative. "The further they are away, the less that the data is actually informing the creative and then we're just making subjective calls."
4. [2:54] **Better team retention.** They see their content succeed: make, see performance, take the insight, make the next one. A "flywheel".
5. [2:54 to 2:55] **The audience has a reason to follow you on more than one platform.** Rule: "Anytime that you're posting the same content at the same time to all the different platforms, you deincentivize your audience to follow you on all these platforms."
6. [2:55 to 2:56] **You build strategists, not just creatives**, and future leaders. Editors with the data in front of them start giving stronger opinions; a few rise and may move into strategy lead or creative director roles.

### 4d. How to decide who gets which platform (team) [2:56 to 2:59]

- [2:56 to 2:57] Basic test: "Does the creator match the tone of the platform?"
- [2:57] TikTok: do they understand current humor and trends? TikTok has its own language.
- [2:57] LinkedIn: do they deeply understand the subject matter you speak on? Someone who does will pull more out of you than someone who only repurposes.
- [2:57 to 2:58] YouTube: "does somebody consume a [lot] of YouTube content?" Someone living on YouTube knows best practices and sees changes as they happen. "What you don't want is somebody who only consumes Netflix and Instagram editing your YouTube content."
- [2:58 to 2:59] Example: an editor who notices vlogs coming back over the last year, and knows a 2025 vlog looks very different from a 2015 one, without leaning on what worked in 2014 or 2015.

### 4e. Data tracking: benchmarks and the multiplier sheet [2:59 to 3:05]

- [2:59] Problem: teams say "this video got 400k views" or "10,000 likes" and celebrate, but nobody knows what it means without the average.
- [3:00] Fix: "establish benchmarks and then... report against those benchmarks."
- [3:00] Named tool: the "multiplier sheet", kept in Google Sheets. Early on it is "directionally correct, not scientifically accurate."
- Steps:
  1. [3:00] Review the last 75 to 90 days of content, per platform.
  2. [3:01] Pull the median. Rule: "Use the median, not the mean." Outlier posts tip the mean and make everything else look like it underperforms.
  3. [3:01] Do it per format (Reels, carousels, static posts). One number across all formats is possible but splitting is more exact.
  4. [3:01 to 3:02] That median is the benchmark. Report each new piece against it "using a multiplier". Example: benchmark 100,000 views on Reels, a post gets 150,000, that is "a 1.5x multiplier."
  5. [3:02] The team then speaks one language: not "this got 150,000 views" but "this was a 1.5x."
  6. [3:02] Tracked over time, it becomes very clear which topics and formats underperform. Make more of what works and less of what does not.
  7. [3:02 to 3:03] Timing rule: "for most platforms waiting 11 days after you post it before you report on the performance." Content may do fine for 2 or 3 days and then pop around day 7. Give it the platform's full life cycle.
  8. [3:03 to 3:04] Share the sheets. A "3x clip" on Instagram is worth testing on TikTok. Multipliers let you compare across platforms: a clip with 150k on Instagram and only 50K on TikTok looks weak, but if the TikTok average is 10,000 it was "a 5x multiplier."
  9. [3:04] Why it is only directionally correct at first: posts in the 75-day window have been up for different lengths of time.
  10. [3:04] Update the benchmark every month so you measure against the new median, not one from 6 months ago. The bar rises with your results instead of someone declaring "we have to hit 500k views."
- [3:05] He would not build this by hand for YouTube because tools already do it. He names two (GARBLED: captioned "one of 10.com" and "view stats"; exact names unverified). He knows of no tool that does it automatically for Instagram or TikTok, so short form is manual.

### 4f. Data as culture, plus two pro tips [3:05 to 3:08]

- [3:05] Make data part of team culture: "everybody is viewing data as their god."
- [3:05] Ratio: "maybe 10% of your content is just new ideas that the team is coming up with, but 90% of it should be backed by data."
- [3:05 to 3:06] (team) Hire people who care about data. Go further and hire someone whose main job includes tracking, analyzing and passing on the takeaways. One such hire on his last team changed the whole culture. "Go for the data nerd."
- [3:06 to 3:07] Pro tip 1, named: the "brick by brick" list. "It's not a boulder that builds a building. It's brick by brick." Write down the bricks you have noticed lead to better content outcomes. It becomes an internal playbook specific to your content, niche and platforms (he will not hand out his own for that reason) and a training tool for new editors. Write it down instead of hoping you remember.
- [3:07 to 3:08] Pro tip 2, rule: "if it doesn't change your actions, don't track it." Big analytics tools tracking every metric cause information overload. Track "two to four metrics that actually indicate what you should do next."

### 4g. No social media manager (team) [3:08 to 3:09]

- [3:08 to 3:09] His polarizing take: the social media manager role is not needed. The people who own each platform should do the posting. They gain more insight and see in real time how their content performs.

### 4h. Build a content machine [3:09 to 3:12]

- [3:09] "Instead of hustling your way to success, build a system and process" so you post consistently and are not "an inconsistent spotty creator."
- **Batching and scheduling** [3:09 to 3:10]
  - If you are very busy, batch.
  - He also believes in "real time content": "You have the idea and within 24 hours you film it." For many viewers that is not realistic.
  - Rule: "give yourself the system that is realistic for you in this current season." For most that is blocking a whole Thursday or Friday to film.
  - A full batch day is exhausting (he is doing one now), so the supportive environment from chapter 1 matters.
- **The content assembly line**, five steps [3:10 to 3:12]
  1. **Ideation**: researching and brainstorming topics.
  2. **Creation**: drafting the script, hitting record, filming.
  3. **Editing**: optimizing the edit per platform (each platform owner cuts for their platform).
  4. **Distribution**: "post and ideally engage." The platform owners post.
  5. **Analyze**: review the performance and note what to improve next time.
- **Who replies to comments** [3:11 to 3:12]
  - His strong recommendation: you, not the team. A viewer values knowing a reply is really you. He would take that "at the detriment of high volume engagement": replying to only five comments a week yourself beats the team replying to every comment as you.
  - Hack (team): a clearly labeled team account. For Gary they ran a "Team Gary Vee" account. When Gary was very busy or about to board a flight without Wi-Fi, the team replied to every comment from that account. No pretending to be Gary.

## 5. Create Room for Experimentation [3:12:56 to 3:20:37]

### 5a. The problem [3:12 to 3:14]

- [3:12] "The biggest mistake that I see in content teams, they stop experimenting."
- [3:13] How it happens: you innovate, learn what works, double down (which is right), then comfort sets in. "The algorithm doesn't get comfortable." He is not saying chase the algorithm, but content is served differently after every change and you must adapt.
- [3:13] Rule: "What got you to where you are now will not get you to the next level." By the time a team notices, it is almost too late.
- [3:13 to 3:14] Admission against his own data-first push: "a lot of times true innovation has no data to back it."
- [3:14] Fix: "bake experimentation into your process", on purpose and on a schedule.
- [3:14] "Most teams aren't failing because of bad ideas. They're failing because they don't have any new ideas."

### 5b. Content hackathons (team) [3:14 to 3:17]

- [3:14] Named practice: "content hackathons". Monthly or quarterly. One whole day to make something completely new and different from daily work.
- How he runs one:
  1. [3:14 to 3:15] 9:00 a.m. kickoff call, 15 to 30 minutes, to give context (new members may have joined).
  2. [3:15] Give a safe space. His words to the team: "I don't care how anything you make today performs. I want you to create something that you've not done before on the team."
  3. [3:15] Make only one or two pieces. Not volume. Aim for something so different "that it's either going to absolutely crush or completely flop. And either way is fine." You are rewarding "innovation, not performance."
  4. [3:15 to 3:16] He pays for lunch (gift cards, or orders in) so nobody has to think about it.
  5. [3:16] Creation window 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m.
  6. [3:16] A shared slide deck, one slide per person. At 4:00 they get 15 to 30 minutes to upload and embed.
  7. [3:16] At 4:30, a 1 to 1.5 hour call. Each person presents and must explain why they did what they did, not only press play.
  8. [3:16 to 3:17] The team votes on the most creative or innovative piece, which is then run publicly. The winner gets a prize such as an Amazon gift card.
- [3:17] Result: "Some of the best content formats that ended up being top performers for months or even years for us came from content hackathons." The team loves it because there is no penalty for a miss.

### 5c. The 70/20/10 framework [3:17 to 3:19]

- [3:18] For ongoing experimentation. "Not every single piece of content should be an experiment. But if you never test new things, you will certainly plateau."
- [3:18] Named framework: "the 70/20/10 framework" (captioned "702010").
  - **70%**: proven content you know works. "The bread and butter." It reliably drives engagement and conversions.
  - **20%**: "small improvements and iterations to the content you know works." Minor tweaks: adjusting the hooks, trying a new call to action, or testing a new format with the same subject matter.
  - **10%**: "pure experimentation. This is where the breakthroughs actually happen." Completely new formats, new topics or messaging, or completely different creative approaches.
- [3:19] Most teams get stuck in the 70% because it is comfortable. "Comfort doesn't build iconic brands." The best teams keep "at least one foot in the 10% zone."
- [3:19] Wrap-up rules: "innovation isn't optional. It's a requirement to scale." Trends shift, platforms change, audiences move on. Without room to experiment you end up "in reaction mode". "The teams that adapt first are the teams that win."

### 5d. The scaling mindset [3:19 to 3:20]

1. So many people quit too early, mostly because they had no process that let them keep going and enjoy it. "The people who win in the content game are the ones who stay consistent and stick with it for a very very long time."
2. "Scaling is about iteration not perfection." Observe, learn, adapt. "Progress over perfection."
3. "Let the audience determine what is great. And the more you post, the more data you get on what your audience says is great."

- [3:20 to 3:21] Transition into the Team section (outside this range): he calls team possibly the most important part; a team is "a multiplier of the vision"; hire more for culture than for skills.

## Exercises he assigns

3. [1:32 to 1:34] Set a cadence you can actually keep (three a week, once a week, once every other week). Start with "whatever you can actually put out consistently."
4. [1:35 to 1:36] Post the imperfect piece, and write down what you will do differently on the next one.
5. [1:36 to 1:41] Identify your pillar content and mine it: self-contained chunks, quotes, a five or six sentence summary, short clips (contrarian or nuanced moments), a LinkedIn post from the transcript, that post reused as an Instagram caption, and two carousel slides per section with hooks on slides one and two.
6. [1:41 to 1:42] Scale one platform at a time; put the others on maintenance mode.
7. [1:46 to 1:48] Monthly: take the last 90 days (or 30), identify the top 10% of posts, and find the one or two things present in all of them. Put those into every piece going forward. Repeat every month.
8. [1:49] Set a baseline output, hold it 2 or 3 months, then raise it.
9. [1:50] Check your strategy against your real calendar: film through the week or batch.
10. [2:05 to 2:06] Find your best-energy time of day and schedule content sessions there. Protect that time like a business meeting.

12. [2:20 to 2:29] Keep telling your origin story as if every viewer is cold. Tell failure stories with the lesson attached, success stories with before and after, client or follower wins, and industry stories where you state the common belief and then your differing view.
13. [2:28] Look at what people in your industry are saying and find where you disagree.
14. [2:35] Post two versions of something you are making and let the audience pick.
15. [2:37 to 2:38] Read your DMs and comments for repeated questions and answer them in content right away, without the "people keep asking me" preamble.

20. [3:07] "Build a list of your bricks": write down each thing you notice that leads to better content outcomes.
21. [3:08] Cut tracking down to the two to four metrics that change what you do next.
22. [3:10 to 3:12] Set up the five-step assembly line: ideation, creation, editing, distribution, analyze. Reply to comments yourself.
23. [3:14 to 3:17] Run a content hackathon monthly or quarterly: a full day, one or two pieces, something never done before, judged on innovation and not performance.
24. [3:18 to 3:19] Split output 70/20/10: proven content, small iterations (new hook, new CTA, new format on the same subject), pure experiments.
