## 2. What Brand Is [0:01 to 0:03]

Definitions, his exact wording [0:01]:
- Branding: "a pairing of things."
- Good branding: "an intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently."
- Brand: the byproduct. "Brand is when the audience inherently associates those things together."

Examples of a pairing that became an association:
- Nike and Michael Jordan [0:01]. Nike wanted to be associated with athletic greatness in basketball, so they "intentionally paired themselves with greatness in basketball."
- Gary Vaynerchuk and content [0:02]. Wrote a book called Crush It ("I think 2009"). "Over time his actions done repetitively have led us to draw that association."
- Harley-Davidson and freedom [0:02]. Years of marketing paired with freedom, the open road, "tearing off their suit and going out and hitting the road."

The working question [0:02]: "what are the associations that we want our audience to inherently draw when they think of our name."

Why this definition is useful [0:03]: "it actually makes it operational for you. All you have to do is deliberately and intentionally pair yourself with the association that you want your audience to make." It takes the "woo woo fluffy magic" out.

## 4. Position Your Brand [0:12 to 0:26]

Goal [0:12]: "Your goal isn't to just blend in. You actually want to find what is missing in your industry and own that gap."

### Find the gap [0:12 to 0:14]
- Three things the gap can be [0:12]:
  1. The information you have.
  2. The philosophy you have around a subject.
  3. Your personality and your unique take on the same information ("You might just be able to reach a whole different audience that wasn't able to connect with some of the other creators in that space").
- "what you are looking for is the gap of what is missing and then you fill it." [0:12]
- The two questions to find it [0:12 to 0:13]:
  1. Of the people currently making content in the space you want to occupy, "What are they saying that I disagree with?"
  2. "How can I take what has been taught for years or shared for years and bring a fresh perspective to the table?"
- Example [0:13]: if everyone in your industry focuses on going viral and views, "maybe leaning into building a long-term trustbuilt brand will stand out."

## 5. Brand Story Framework [0:26 to 0:33]

Framing [0:26]:
- "Your brand story isn't just a before and after transformation. It's a series of intentional decisions that shape how people see you."
- Why he rejects most story frameworks: "a little too rigid." They assume three things [0:26]:
  1. every brand has a dramatic aha moment or one big problem to solve
  2. the founder story is the most important one
  3. the brand's identity will never evolve
- Rule [0:26 to 0:27]: "great brands aren't built on a singular moment. They're built on consistency, distinction, and connecting with the right audience over time."
- "Forget the problem transformation outcome framework." He says it is too narrow [0:27].

Named framework: "the threepart brand story framework" [0:27]. Each part has three questions.

### Part 1: The Catalyst [0:27 to 0:28]
- Definition: "This is why your brand exists." "Every brand, I believe, starts because something needed to change."
- It is not always a struggle. "It might be an opportunity or a gap in the market."
- Example (his assumption, he says he does not know it for sure): Phil Knight did not start Nike just to get rich; he saw no other company meeting the market's needs [0:27].
- Three questions [0:27 to 0:28]:
  1. "What needs to change?"
  2. "What do you see that others don't?" (the opportunity you see clearly that nobody else can)
  3. "why do you feel the need to act on it?"

### Part 2: The Core Truth [0:28 to 0:30]
- Definition: "This is what makes you or your company different than everybody else in your space."
- Rule [0:28]: "A strong brand stands out. It doesn't blend in. Blending in ultimately leads to you being forgotten."
- What it is not [0:28]: controversy for its own sake. "That's where you just get the annoyingly loud people on the internet that nobody likes."
- What it is [0:28]: "having a core conviction that is different than everyone else in the market and not only having that core conviction but sharing it publicly with the world."
- Example [0:28 to 0:29]: the artist Russ. Strong view that artists should stay independent and own the rights to their music (creative control, more profit). He puts that belief in the music itself and repeats it in podcast and news interviews.
- Three questions [0:29 to 0:30]:
  1. "What do I believe that others do not?"
  2. "What is it about my personality that stands out?" Paired with: "what do people compliment you on that you can lean into, that you can double down on?" (his energy and personality example again)
  3. "why would the audience care about this? How does this impact them? What value does it bring for them?"

### Part 3: The Proof [0:30 to 0:31]
- Definition: "This is how you reinforce your identity over and over and over."
- Rule [0:30]: "your brand is not what you say, it's what you do and prove over and over again."
- Rule [0:30]: "Strong brands don't just have one past success story. They have an ongoing pattern of credibility."
- Example [0:30 to 0:31]: Gary does not just talk about volume of content, "He's proved it for like 15 plus years," and backs it with stories: his own brand, and VaynerMedia's Super Bowl campaigns, most of which are decided from high-volume social content run through the year to test what the audience resonates with.
- Three questions [0:31]:
  1. "how does every piece of content I make reinforce the associations I want people to make with me?"
  2. "What case studies or examples can I share that establish my credibility?"
  3. "if someone hears your name, what's the first thing that they think of?"

### His own filled-in example [0:31 to 0:32]
- Catalyst: he saw brands trying to scale on views and impressions alone but struggling to build trust. The content was not the problem; they lacked clarity on big-picture strategy and what they wanted from the brand.
- Core truth: "a strong brand is built by the intentional pairing of relevant things done consistently, not just getting virality."
- Proof: he has built many brands that scaled businesses to millions in revenue, and shows how for free in his content.

### One-line version of the three steps [0:32 to 0:33]
- "The catalyst. What do you see that others do not?"
- "The core truth. What do you believe that others might not believe?"
- "the proof. How do you continually reinforce that identity every single day? Not only through what you're saying, but through your actions."
- Payoff [0:33]: "If you nail these, your brand won't just have a story. Your brand will have loyalty."

## 8. Choose Your Content Medium [0:52 to 1:08]

- Definition [0:52]: the medium is "the type of content, the style of content that you are going to emphasize."
- He thinks you should do all of them, but decide "what is going to be your 80%. What are you going to lean into the most?" [0:52 to 0:53]
- Rule [0:53]: "Picking the right content medium is about playing to your strengths while ensuring long-term sustainability."
- The four mediums [0:53]: written, video, audio, graphic/design.

### Written [0:53 to 0:56]
- Best for [0:53]: LinkedIn, Twitter/X, Facebook, email, newsletters, ebooks, articles.
- Who it suits [0:53]: someone who can take high-value, dense information and write it "concise and high value per sentence."
- Measure [0:53]: for video he thinks "value per second"; for written, "value per word or value per sentence."
- LinkedIn as of February 2025 [0:54]: "in my opinion, the hottest platform to be on" for entrepreneurs, creators, musicians, artists. "super underrated." It is no longer just a resume site; people post family road trip photos next to career updates, behaving the way they did on Facebook in "2013 2014." "if you comment on somebody's post all of their connections see it," so it has "true virality."
- "written content is murdering on LinkedIn if you understand how to package and format your written post if you get the hook right." [0:55]
- Questions to ask yourself [0:55 to 0:56]:
  1. "Do you enjoy sitting down and writing or even journaling? Are you already doing this?" If you journal daily you may already have "maybe 70% of the skills required" and "100% of the interest."
  2. "can you write this down, this concept, and have it make sense without saying it out loud?" Rule [0:56]: "If you have to explain it or add commentary, it is not a good post and you should throw it in the trash."
  3. "do you like the idea of refining it, editing it, reviewing it?" His number [0:56]: about "10 different versions minimum" of a LinkedIn post.
- Comparison [0:56]: "as an editor, you have to be comfortable watching the same moment hundreds of times." Editing written content is the same.

### Video [0:56 to 1:00]
- Rule [0:56 to 0:57]: "Video content is the highest leverage medium that you can engage in."
- What one filmed video yields [0:57], [0:59]:
  1. the main (long form) video
  2. short form clips pulled from it
  3. the audio, as a podcast
  4. punchy quotes from the transcript, for Threads, Twitter, or a Facebook status as an image with the copy on it
  5. the transcript as the starting point for LinkedIn posts, articles or a newsletter
  6. stills pulled from the video, for social posts [0:59]
- "you put the effort of filming this and then you're able to redistribute or repurpose that content for so many different mediums for so many different platforms." [0:57]
- If the idea of being on camera makes you shudder: "don't do it yet." [0:57] "we want to create a system that we enjoy and we look forward to, not something that we dread and get anxiety when we see it on the calendar." [0:58]
- Where video goes [0:58]: YouTube long form, YouTube short form, TikTok, Instagram, LinkedIn video tab, Facebook Reels, Spotify video. "everything is pretty much pushing video these days."
- Who it is best for [0:58]: someone comfortable in front of the camera who can articulate fairly clearly, or someone not a natural but "willing to improve and work on it in real time in public like myself."
- "the amount that you can get for the amount you put in is limitless." [0:59]
- Questions to ask yourself [0:59]:
  1. "are you comfortable on camera or are you at least willing to try and improve in public over time?"
  2. "Can you handle negative feedback from people calling out your lack of abilities to articulate or be entertaining on camera?"
  3. "Do you get excited about visuals and grabbing attention?"
  4. "can you speak concisely without rambling?" Or, if not, are you comfortable learning on the job, in public.
- Named term [1:00]: "bunny brain." When you know a subject very well, you start saying one thing, the brain fires off the next idea, and you follow "this little rabbit trail." It makes you ramble and go off topic.
- Rule [1:00]: that "is not necessarily a reason to not do video content. That just means you need to have a better editor that can help make sure that the content stays focused."

### Audio [1:00 to 1:05]
- Mainly podcasts [1:00].
- "it's exactly the same as video except for you remove the discomfort of staring into this lens." [1:00] Most people have no cameraman behind the lens nodding along (he has Trevor) [1:00 to 1:01].
- Recommendation [1:01]: "if the idea of video is exciting and terrifying at the same time, maybe consider for a period of time starting with a podcast." People who do this soon see that video is doable and "very high reward."
- Questions to ask yourself [1:02 to 1:03]:
  1. "do you feel confident speaking without a visual element?" With no visual aid "you have to use your words to be able to articulate and paint the picture." "it's not like podcasting is just easier than video." Descriptions have to be "even more clear."
  2. "do you enjoy deeper conversations or deeper dives into one specific subject?" He prefers longer podcasts; he says the majority of the top 100 are "a little bit longer than 20 minutes" [1:03].
  3. "does your voice have enough energy and clarity to keep people engaged?" [1:03]
- On voice [1:03 to 1:04]: cadence does the work. He talks fast then slows down, louder then quieter, to mark emphasis. "It's a pattern interrupt."
- His life philosophy, stated here [1:04]: "taking whatever is given to you and using it as your advantage, even if it was viewed as a disadvantage by most people."
- Applied [1:04]: "If you have a monotone voice, I would actually lean into that and I would make that your bit." Some of his favorite podcasts and comedians are monotone and sarcastic.
- Rule [1:04 to 1:05]: "whatever you see right now as the reason why you shouldn't do it, I would argue is probably the very reason why you should do it."

### Visual / graphic [1:05 to 1:07]
- What it is [1:05]: infographics, carousels, PDFs uploaded to LinkedIn as a carousel ("That's kind of the hack there"), anything design-heavy.
- Not only for designers [1:05]: tools named are Canva, Figma, Adobe programs, AI. You can also use a designer; "It doesn't always have to be you implementing it."
- Who it suits [1:05 to 1:06]: people who do not like video but want to explain their thinking with visuals as well as words.
- As of February 2025 [1:06]: "Carousels are murdering" on Instagram. "the simpler the better." A carousel pairs a visual with the written word, two ways to explain one concept.
- Questions to ask yourself [1:06 to 1:07]:
  1. "are you good at or curious about tools like" Canva, Adobe, Figma?
  2. "can you simplify complex concepts into a visual?" Example: an Instagram account, "I believe it's called Visualize Value," that turns complex concepts into very simple visuals.
  3. "do you enjoy the process of making content look great, looking polished?" He adds this is not required.
- Side note [1:07]: "something that is currently working really well online is drawing something or creating something physical in the real world, taking a photo of it, and uploading it."

### Closing the medium choice [1:07 to 1:08]
- He is doing all four and encourages it "If you feel like you have either the team around you, bandwidth, or skill set, or ideally all three."
- Choosing only one is fine if all four feels like too much at the start. "We're trying to do one thing at a time to start to gain momentum and stick with this for a long time." [1:08]

## 9. Choosing the Right Platforms [1:08 to 1:23]

### Two to three platforms [1:08 to 1:10]
- Rule [1:08]: "prioritize two to three platforms." He discourages "post everywhere, post all the time."
- Why [1:08]: you gain more by picking, say, two platforms "that you're going to learn heavily and really emphasize original content for making content specific for that platform."
- Why not one [1:09]: "you want to avoid single channel, single platform risk."
- Example [1:09]: a month before filming there was a 12-hour period when TikTok disappeared in the US. The people who panicked were those whose income was tied only to TikTok and who never built elsewhere.
- He does not expect apps to be banned, but "algorithms change" [1:09 to 1:10]. Instagram tweaks constantly, and differently per person: "You and I are getting different updates at different times. You have features available to you that I do not have and vice versa."
- Rule [1:10]: do not post on all platforms, but "make sure that you aren't tied to one single platform."

### The Eye of Sauron approach [1:10 to 1:11]
- Named framework: "the Eye of Sauron approach." [1:10]
- Of the two or three platforms, put "more emphasis or more priority and resources towards one at a time," and rotate. The others sit "on maintenance mode" while one is the focus (his example: focus YouTube, then put YouTube in maintenance mode and move to LinkedIn).

### What he did himself [1:11 to 1:13]
- First time he is making consistent content for himself rather than for talent [1:11].
- His counts [1:11]: under about 4,000 followers on YouTube, TikTok, X, Threads, most under 2,000; about 68,000 on Instagram.
- He picked four platforms, while telling you to pick two or three. "Do more of what I say in this, not as much of what I do." (He cites his 16 years.) [1:11]
- He started with LinkedIn, not Instagram, because he believed most of his potential clients were there, and because he felt most comfortable with LinkedIn and Instagram. A couple of LinkedIn posts first, then an Instagram post [1:11 to 1:12].
- Rule [1:12]: "Start where you feel comfortable." On "get out of your comfort zone": "Not in the beginning. In the beginning, you want to build the habit of doing it."
- Gym example [1:12]: he told friends starting out to "go to the gym for 10 minutes 5 days a week." Building the habit of driving there is usually the real issue.
- Rule [1:12]: "Build the habit of making and posting your content. Make it as easy as possible."
- His expansion [1:12 to 1:13]: from LinkedIn and Instagram to YouTube long form and a podcast.

### Short form vs long form [1:13 to 1:14]
- Rule [1:13]: "short form content is amazing to drive awareness" and to get brand recognition. "I view long form as the place where conversion actually happens. This is where we're able to truly demonstrate our excellence and expertise."
- For someone deciding [1:13]: "short form has a lower barrier to entry. So, it allows you to start to get the repetitions in" and build the habit. "eventually I would encourage you to implement one or two long form styles. either YouTube long form video or long form podcasts, ideally both."
- Why, two reasons [1:13 to 1:14]:
  1. The more time someone spends with you, the more they see your expertise.
  2. "sunk cost fallacy": "the more time somebody spends with you, the more willing they are to actually act on an offer that you present them."
- Harley-Davidson dealership example [1:14]: the finance team has your credit result within seconds but keeps you 10 or 15 minutes with the sales associate, then walks you through the whole dealership, so you invest time and do not want to waste it.
- Rule [1:14]: "The more time your audience spends with you, the more likely they are to transact or convert on your offer."

### Questions for picking the platform [1:14 to 1:17]
1. Where are you already comfortable or active [1:14 to 1:15]. "Start where you're comfortable. If you're already active on LinkedIn, Instagram, YouTube, Tik Tok, wherever, then I would just use that to start."
   - He stresses posting, not just making [1:15]: "a lot of you make and then it sits on your photo album on your phone and you never actually put it out."
2. "where do you naturally spend the most of your time?" [1:15] If you scroll TikTok a lot you likely know what does well there. Turn doom scrolling into study: ask "why did I like watching this video?", take notes, and keep a list of best practices to use in your own content.
3. "where is your audience?" [1:15 to 1:16] Go back to the brand journey framework: "based on what our desired outcome is, who are we needing to reach in order for that to occur? And where do we believe (it's a hypothesis) that they exist or they live the most?" His case: a consulting firm for entrepreneurs, creators and entertainers, who are active on LinkedIn, so his hypothesis was more qualified leads there than on Instagram.
4. What are they there for [1:16]: "are they on Tik Tok for quick engaging content or are they on YouTube for more long [form] deep dives into one specific subject matter or are they around on LinkedIn trying to be one of those thought leaders."
- Rule [1:16]: "make an educated guess on where you think your audience is and then test it, monitor it." Check: "Are you getting actual leads? Are people reaching out to you inquiring about what you do?"
- If not, two options [1:17]: "One, your content sucks and nobody" cares (caption drops a word). "Two, maybe you picked the wrong platform."

### Double down on what is working [1:17 to 1:18]
- Once you know what you are skilled at, double down on it [1:17]. (Caption drops a word in "double the down on it.")
- Example [1:17]: if you chose video and written, and video is not performing while written is, "not to eliminate video, but reduce the amount of effort you put towards your video content and increase the amount for the written content."
- Rule [1:17]: "when you are popping on a platform, that is not the time in my opinion to go about trying to level up other skill sets. That is the time where you double down and you pour gas on the thing that you are actually good at and can consistently put out that your audience deems as high quality and high value."
- If you are confident on video, double down on short form or long form video. "whatever you feel you are best at, you should be leaning into that heavily." If you prefer audio, start a podcast; you will likely get comfortable with video later, which is the "higher leverage activity" [1:18].

### Results over ego [1:18 to 1:19]
- Rule [1:18]: "look at your results and don't listen to your ego."
- His own admission [1:18]: watching the footage from this course he feels he looks fat. He says to push past that. If the content is getting traction and people are resonating, how you think you look does not matter; viewers likely are not thinking about it.
- "If I allowed my ego to dictate what we did, we would probably not release any of the content we've made so far." [1:19]
- Rule [1:19]: "look and track your metrics. Look at the engagement. Look at the results you're getting." Stop listening to the inner voice about how weird your voice sounds or how tired you look; it "is not what is propelling you towards your goals."

### Small correct audience beats a big wrong one [1:19 to 1:21]
- Rule [1:19]: "a small correct audience that is engaged is so much more powerful than having a massive audience of people that have no idea what" you are doing and do not care. (Caption drops words.)
- His example [1:19 to 1:20]: 68,000 Instagram followers, gained about 10 years ago from Pacific Northwest landscape photography, not engaged with him now. He will have to convert them to the new content and expects to lose a lot of them.
- Directly relevant number [1:20]: "a lot of you watching this probably have 500 to a thousand followers, but those followers might actually be very engaged in your subject matter and very interested in the offer you have."
- He knows people with millions of followers who make very little from social, and people with fewer than 10,000 followers making millions a year. "Why? Because those 5,000 people that follow them are their target audience, their potential customers." [1:20] (Note: he says "less than 10,000" and then "those 5,000" in the same passage.)
- Rule [1:20 to 1:21]: do not chase the inflated follower count that impresses friends. "You want to make sure that you are growing and acquiring the correct audience." "please do not be discouraged if you don't have hundreds of thousands or millions of followers."

### Platform performance AND conversions [1:21 to 1:23]
- Track performance on the platform [1:21]: it is "an indicator that you are making content that the platform prefers and so people will see it and engage with it." For early solo operators and creators, "This is how people are aware of your offer." "it's important for you to play the game."
- Rule [1:21]: "actually pay more attention to conversions."
- His four conversion examples [1:21 to 1:22]:
  1. Musician: are more people streaming your music?
  2. Clothing brand: are more people buying your hoodies?
  3. Video editor: are more people hiring you for gigs?
  4. Investor: are more companies coming to you rather than you reaching out?
- The "huge mistake" [1:22]: people "build their whole brand and content based on performing well on Tik Tok, Instagram or YouTube and they never build it based on actually converting to sales or to streams."
- Rule [1:22]: "You're trying to drive awareness that leads to a desired outcome. And if you don't optimize around the desired outcome," you "waste one, two, maybe three years and lots of money and time chasing some vanity metric that doesn't actually improve your bottom line."
- Rule [1:22 to 1:23]: "The key here is not to think of it as one or the other. It's both." Watch platform performance so people become aware of the offer, and optimize the content so it leads to action.
- His stated preference, with numbers [1:23]: "I would rather have 30 40% less likes and views and subscribers or followers, but have that audience convert at a higher percentage than have millions of followers and have a very low percentage of conversion."
- His goal [1:23]: not fame. "My goal is purely to become known with my potential customers." "I don't need everybody to know who I am."
- Rule [1:23]: "make sure that you are mapping and building out your strategy and optimizing your content for the correct thing."

## 10. Your Posting Cadence, opening only [1:23 to 1:24]

Only the first minute falls in this part; the framework itself is in the next part.
- He says he got the cadence question "at least 300 times in the last year" [1:23 to 1:24].
- What is coming [1:24]: a framework for determining your posting cadence, then another framework for how to increase volume and cadence, and before both, his "overarching philosophy on quality versus quantity."
- His complaint about the debate [1:24]: it has run about eight years online, and people in it "don't actually define what these two things mean and what they are and what their purpose is."

## Exercises he assigns

These are the questions and tasks he tells the viewer to answer for their own brand, in the order he gives them. Wording is his where quoted.

### A. Brand Journey Framework [0:04]
1. "What is our desired outcome? What do we want to have happen?"
2. "What do I have to be known for in order for that to happen?"
3. "What do I have to do in order to be known for that?"
4. "What do I have to learn in order to do that?"

### B. Associations [0:06 to 0:07]
5. "What do I want to be associated with? What do I want the audience to think of when they hear my name?"
6. "What do you not want to be associated with?" (He rates this above question 5.)

### C. Positioning: the gap [0:12 to 0:13]
7. Of the people making content in the space I want to occupy: "What are they saying that I disagree with?"
8. "How can I take what has been taught for years or shared for years and bring a fresh perspective to the table?"

### D. Positioning: your story [0:14 to 0:15]
9. "What is different about your story than everybody else's?" and "what is it that makes you uniquely you?"
10. List out: pivotal moments in your story and life; challenges you have overcome; turning points in your career; "any unique experiences that have shaped your perspective."
11. For each trait you would claim (hardworking, resourceful), find the story that shows it and tell the story instead [0:15 to 0:16].

### E. Positioning: objections [0:17]
12. Read your comment section, list the skepticism and objections that keep coming up, and answer them inside the content before they are asked.

### F. Positioning: be human [0:18 to 0:21]
13. Share losses and failures along with "the lessons that you learned from those failures," not only wins.

### G. Positioning: listen to your audience [0:22 to 0:23]
14. Ask of friends, co-workers and any audience you already have: "What are they associating you with? What do they praise you for? What do they applaud you for? What do they admire in you" and "what keeps them coming back?" Use the answers to refine positioning and decide what to double down on.

### H. Three-part Brand Story Framework [0:27 to 0:31]
Catalyst:
15. "What needs to change?"
16. "What do you see that others don't?"
17. "Why do you feel the need to act on it?"

Core truth:
18. "What do I believe that others do not?"
19. "What is it about my personality that stands out?" and "what do people compliment you on that you can lean into, that you can double down on?"
20. "Why would the audience care about this? How does this impact them? What value does it bring for them?"

Proof:
21. "How does every piece of content I make reinforce the associations I want people to make with me?"
22. "What case studies or examples can I share that establish my credibility?"
23. "If someone hears your name, what's the first thing that they think of?"

### I. Topics [0:45 to 0:47]
24. "What am I genuinely passionate about discussing?"
25. "What will resonate with my audience most right now?"
26. "What will cause my audience to change their actions the most?" (He calls this the most important of the three.)
27. Split the pie: name the one main thing for the 80%, and the interests that fill the 20% (interest stacking) [0:47 to 0:50].
28. Before adding a topic, check: do I actually know it at a high level, am I already known for the first topic with consistent engagement, is the audience asking for it, and is it adjacent ("layers, not leaps") [0:36 to 0:43].

### J. Content medium [0:52 to 1:07]
29. Overall: "what do I feel like I can actually consistently stick with" and which medium is "your 80%."
30. Written: "Do you enjoy sitting down and writing or even journaling? Are you already doing this?"; "can you write this down, this concept, and have it make sense without saying it out loud?"; "do you like the idea of refining it, editing it, reviewing it?"
31. Video: "are you comfortable on camera or are you at least willing to try and improve in public over time?"; "Can you handle negative feedback from people calling out your lack of abilities to articulate or be entertaining on camera?"; "Do you get excited about visuals and grabbing attention?"; "can you speak concisely without rambling?"
32. Audio: "do you feel confident speaking without a visual element?"; "do you enjoy deeper conversations or deeper dives into one specific subject?"; "does your voice have enough energy and clarity to keep people engaged?"
33. Visual/graphic: "are you good at or curious about tools like" Canva, Adobe, Figma; "can you simplify complex concepts into a visual?"; "do you enjoy the process of making content look great, looking polished?"

### K. Platforms [1:08 to 1:23]
34. Pick two to three platforms; pick which one gets the focus now and which sit in maintenance mode (Eye of Sauron) [1:08 to 1:10].
35. Where are you already comfortable or active? Start there [1:14].
36. "Where do you naturally spend the most of your time?" While scrolling there, ask "why did I like watching this video?" and take notes on best practices [1:15].
37. "Where is your audience?" Based on the desired outcome, "who are we needing to reach in order for that to occur" and where do they live most (a hypothesis) [1:15 to 1:16].
38. Are they on TikTok for quick content, on YouTube for deep dives, or on LinkedIn [1:16].
39. Test the guess: "Are you getting actual leads? Are people reaching out to you inquiring about what you do?" If not, either the content is weak or the platform is wrong [1:16 to 1:17].
40. Track both platform performance and conversions toward the desired outcome, and weight conversions more [1:21 to 1:23].
