If depression had a capital, it would be this city built by the prisoners of a communist system. It's 1931, Northern Russia, an area full of coal buried deep underground. The Soviet Union needs fuel, so they send trains packed with prisoners into the Arctic. Their job, build a mine, then a town around it. The coal feeds Moscow. The camp grows, barracks become blocks. The mine becomes a city. It's cold, brutal, but it works. By the 1950s, it's one of the biggest coal producers in the USSR. Tens of thousands live here, not because they want to, because they have to. Then the Soviet Union collapses. And everything built on fear starts falling apart. Today, what's left of it barely survives. The mines that fed an empire are silent, out of 50 districts, only a handful still have people. The rest are ghost zones, abandoned schools, frozen buses, streets buried in snow. Temperatures drop below minus 50 degrees Celsius in winter. And even in summer, the ground never melts. The average worker earns less than half the Russian national wage. Trains rarely come, planes are grounded for weeks by storms. And it's name, Vorkuta.
