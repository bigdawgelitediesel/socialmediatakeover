## 2. The format, measured (the history era, which is the one that worked)

Structure of a 60 to 80 s episode, consistent across the five read:
1. **Hook sentence is a flat superlative with a number or a stake in the first 3 s**, spoken and burned in 3 to 5 word chunks: "This country built the most expensive and corrupt sporting event in history, and over 6,000 workers lost their lives." "If you ever thought your job was bad, just remember, at least you weren't a worker on the Great Wall of China." "If depression had a capital, it would be this city." "An African king once defeated the greatest army on earth using nothing but spears and perfect geometry."
2. **Date and place in sentence two.** "It's 220 BC." "It was 1879 in South Africa." "It's 1931, Northern Russia."
3. **One shot per clause, 1.5 to 3 s each, 25 to 40 shots per episode.** Every shot is a new generated image or short clip; the camera barely moves inside a shot (slow push, slow drift). The edit is the motion.
4. **Captions:** white heavy rounded sans (Luckiest Guy style), caps, 2 to 5 words, centred lower third, thin dark shadow, no box. Key numbers sometimes in a colour. Occasional full-black card with the hook phrase in big type (Great Wall at 4 to 7 s, "POV: you were wounded during the Vietnam War").
5. **Voice:** his own, dry, fast, present tense, 150 to 170 wpm, no music under the hook, a low cinematic bed after. The 30-second facts are one breath per shot.
6. **Ending:** the last line is the twist or the name ("And its name, Vorkuta." "She called him dignified." "Nobody knows exactly where."). No outro. An "want to make videos like this, link in bio" pill sits in the frame for 4 s mid-video on the early ones.
7. **Cadence:** one a day for the first six weeks.

## 3. The look (what "the style" actually is)

He does not have one style; he has one process and swaps the look per series. The site lists ten: cinematic realism, graphic novel, 90s cartoon noir, anime action, animated noir, painted fantasy, bold cartoon warrior, ink noir, retro illustration, 80s anime sci-fi. In the ten videos read:

- **Stylised 3D cartoon, teal and orange, lantern light** (Great Wall, Vietnam POV, Dunkirk): exaggerated faces, big eyes, a Samurai Jack flatness in the Vietnam one, strong rim light, dark backgrounds, fire as the accent colour. This is the look of the 4M post.
- **Flat mid-century poster illustration** (Zulu, 593K): square frame inside the vertical, paper texture, two or three inks, text inside the picture.
- **Photoreal-leaning CG** (Berlin Wall 623K, mummification 1.8M): Pixar-adjacent faces, real-looking textures, top-down and macro shots, one continuous location.
- **Painterly 2D** (the hunter and the raptor on Instagram): rough brushwork, deep green jungle, warm earth tones, from Pinterest references.
- **Photoreal live-action look with himself as the lead** (Grok Odyssey, 727K): he inserted his own face as the main character.

Common to all: a limited palette (two colours plus black), a single light source, characters with the same face across shots (character sheets), and no on-screen UI except the captions.

## 4. The pipeline, as he teaches it

From the Instagram reel and the academy page:
1. **References.** A Pinterest board of 6 to 10 images for the look (brushwork, light, palette). Module 2: find a style on Pinterest and Instagram, rebuild it in Midjourney, refine until it is yours.
2. **Stills.** GPT Image (or Midjourney) with the references attached: a character sheet per character (the hunter, the raptor) and the environment, all in the same style. Consistency comes from reusing the same references and the same prompt skeleton.
3. **Motion.** Seedance 2.5 or Kling with the character stills and the environment as references and one prompt per shot or per sequence ("the whole hunt from the stare down to the moment the tribe bows"). Module 3 is prompting for motion, which he calls the part free tutorials skip.
4. **Voice and sound.** His own narration recorded first; the cuts follow the words. Music added last ("music was the last thing I fixed in my pipeline").
5. **Edit.** Shots cut on the clauses, captions burned per 2 to 5 words, one black card for the hook when needed. Module 4 is "the editing that stops it looking like AI."
6. **Ideas.** ChatGPT and Claude for story and hook ideation (Module 6).
7. **Budget.** "Three tools, under $40 a month." The three are an image model, a video model and an editor; the newer sponsored videos push invideo Agent One as the all-in-one, and Luma and Higgsfield appear as partners.

## 5. If Jacob ever does a garage mini series (held, not planned)

What it would and would not be, so the decision is clean when it comes:

- **The thing that worked for him is a story with a number in sentence one, told in 60 to 80 s, one shot per clause, dry voice, no tool talk.** A garage series would be the same shape: "The $50 part that took a truck down", "The five stages of a backorder", "It just started doing that today", told by a cartoon parts guy and a cartoon customer. The scripts already exist in the plan (ideas 44, 53, 47); the animation would be a second rendering of them, not new material.
- **A fixed cast and one look.** El Coyote (a coyote in a shop shirt), the customer, the tech, the owner-op, the truck. Character sheets once, reused forever. One palette: shop fluorescent green-white, oxblood, tan, black. The repo note stands: a coyote mark should be drawn and approved before it appears anywhere.
- **Rules that would still apply:** Meta's AI label on every one; the "real footage only" rule gets a written exception for the series and nothing else; no customer or coworker likeness without asking; El Coyote and the shop can appear (2026-11-22 rule); no delete or coal jokes even in cartoon form.
- **Cost and time, honest:** his claim is under $40 a month and about 20 minutes of machine time per episode once the character sheets exist, plus the script and voice, which Jacob already does for the talking heads. The first two weeks are the expensive part (building the cast and the look). Expect ten failed generations per usable shot at the start.
- **The risk his own graph shows:** the audience that comes for an animated short is not the audience that comes for a man in a truck. He grew to a million on stories and lost 95 percent of his views when the videos became about the tool. A garage cartoon would be a separate lane, probably a separate account, judged on its own numbers, and never the thing the community is built on.
- **Where it could earn a spot without an account of its own:** a ten-second animated cold open or sting on the funny talking heads (the cartoon customer says the line, cut to Jacob in the truck reacting). That is a signature, not a series, and it tests the look for almost nothing.
