A yap is a Reel where one person talks straight into the camera about one idea, and the words carry the whole thing. It is the format behind Oren John's creative direction essays, Jessi Jean's Yap Challenge, Jun Yuh's storytelling reels and most of what parents watch from Dr. Becky. Every guide to it says the same three things: be yourself, use good lighting, talk like you are on FaceTime. None of them quotes a script.
So we read the scripts. On October 3, 2026 we pasted 84 public talking-head Reels from 17 creators into the 
Instagram Reel script extractor
 and put the transcripts side by side. The set covers 16 Reels from Oren John, 13 from Dr. Becky, 11 from Jun Yuh, 10 from Jessi Jean, 8 from Codie Sanchez, 9 from Brian Mark, 4 from Alex Hormozi, 3 from Chris Do, and 10 from nine tech and career accounts that came up in a search for the "10 things that get increasingly niche" format. Clips under 30 words were left out. We quote short lines only.
1. Nobody says hello
Zero of the 84 Reels open by greeting the audience. Two start with the word "hey", and both are Brian Mark quoting a DM ("Hey Brian, what do I say in the DMs to book a sales call?"). The rest start on the idea.
Jun Yuh says why in one of his own Reels: "No one is watching your videos with a group of friends. So stop saying, hey guys or hi everyone."
The headline numbers from the 84 scripts.
2. The first sentence is 13 words long, and it is usually about you
The median first sentence runs 13 words. In 34 of the 84 Reels it contains "you" or "your". In 17 it contains a number. In 14 it is a question. Some first lines from the set, each one a complete thought:
"We used to live our hobbies. Now, we just buy into them." (Oren John)
"Stop posting more. I mean it." (Jun Yuh)
"Money loves speed." (Codie Sanchez)
"My kid never takes accountability. It's always someone else's fault." (Dr. Becky)
"Being a content creator isn't a real job, or so they say." (Jessi Jean)
"Four words to never say in marketing." (Brian Mark)
"Strategy is a word for war." (Oren John)
"If I woke up with zero dollars tomorrow and wanted to become a millionaire" (Alex Hormozi)
Each one states the claim or names the exact situation, and the rest of the Reel earns it.

3. A yap is about 270 words
The median transcript is 271 words. Half of the Reels sit between 191 and 326 words. The shortest substantial one is Brian Mark's four-word list at 37 words, and the longest is Alex Hormozi's millionaire roadmap at just over 600. We did not time the videos, but at a normal talking pace of around 150 words a minute, 270 words is close to two minutes of speech.
The biggest Reels in the set are shorter than the median. Codie Sanchez's haters Reel is 109 words and took 240,026 likes. Her three businesses Reel is 157 words and took 246,331. Jun Yuh's "10 out of 10 habits" is 235 words and took 395,841. Length does not seem to hurt, but the ones people share most say one thing and stop.

4. Half of them are lists said out loud
44 of the 84 transcripts contain "number one", "first", "second" or "step one". The list is the most common skeleton in the set by a wide margin, and it shows up in every niche: three businesses to buy, five hires, seven Instagram settings, three career takes, ten habits that get increasingly niche.
A list gives the viewer a reason to stay (how many are there, how niche does it get), it gives each item a chance to be the one they comment on, and it is easy to say without a script. The 
"10 things that get increasingly niche" format
 is the purest version, and it has been copied from Jun Yuh into tech accounts within weeks.
5. Twice as much "you" as "I"
Across the set, "you" and "your" appear 4.9 times per 100 words and "I", "my" and "I'm" appear 2.6 times. The median sentence is 12 words. Creators who sound like they are talking about themselves are mostly talking about the viewer, with their own story used as proof in the middle.
Jessi Jean is the exception. Her most-liked Reel in our set is an unscripted update on how she feels after a big launch, told almost entirely in the first person, and it took 15,206 likes against a feed that usually sits around 5,000. So "I" works when the Reel is a story, and "you" works when it gives advice.

6. The ask lives in the caption
Few of the 84 Reels end on a question to the audience: counted strictly, only 2 finish on a question mark, and a handful more ask something in their last lines. 14 say "comment" out loud and 15 say "follow". The rest end on the last point, and the sell happens in the caption with a keyword (OWN, STORY, GAME, DFK) that a DM tool answers.
That split keeps the Reel itself clean. Dr. Becky says "More in the caption" and stops. Codie Sanchez never pitches in the script and sells the book in the caption instead. Brian Mark is the exception, with "comment GAME" inside the video, and his Reels are also the shortest and most direct in the set.
How the Reels end. Most just stop, and the sell lives in the caption.
7. A named idea beats a tip
11 of the Reels give the idea a name before explaining it: deeply feeling kids, the storytelling wheel, emotional vaccination, the buyback loop, the Closer framework, gear acquisition syndrome, visual counter-positioning. A tip is forgettable, while a name can be searched, repeated in a comment, and sold later.
Oren John's feed: a thesis on every cover. Screenshot: Instagram.
The shape, in six moves
Put the findings together and most of the 84 Reels follow the same six moves. Not every Reel uses all six, but the order barely changes.
The line.
 One sentence, around 13 words, with the claim or the viewer's own moment in it.
The mistake.
 What people do now and why it backfires, said in the second person.
The name.
 The idea gets a name, a letter, or a number, and a one-sentence definition.
The proof.
 A list, a short story with a number in it, or a named example.
The turn.
 The one sentence the viewer is meant to repeat. Usually a reversal: "You don't get rich doing more. You get rich doing less of the things that don't actually make you any money."
The exit.
 A short instruction, a dare for the comments, or nothing. The sell goes in the caption.

A yap script you can fill in
Eight lines, in our words, shaped like the set. Say it out loud before you record and cut any line that sounds written.
[The claim, or the exact moment your viewer is in, in under 13 words.]
Most people [the common move]. It feels right. It backfires because [one reason].
I call this [a name]. [One sentence that defines it.]
First, [item]. [One reason.] Second, [item]. [One reason.] Third, [item]. [One reason.]
I know because [one moment from your own life, with a number or a date in it].
[The turn: the sentence someone could repeat to a friend.]
[One instruction for today.]
[Stop. Put the offer and the keyword in the caption.]
