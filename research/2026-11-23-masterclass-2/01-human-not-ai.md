# 01. Human, not AI: what reads as machine-made, what reads as a person, sameness fatigue, and growing through people

Written 2026-11-23 for the second masterclass run. Jacob's brief: "Study how to make video less AI and connect to more human growers. Make a streamline editor that challenges its own ideas so nothing looks the same." This file extends sections 1, 4, 5 and 6 of `research/2026-11-22-REEL-EDITOR-DOCTRINE.md` (change not cut, delivery as a human, the hold, when to cut) and does not repeat them. Where the doctrine already has a rule (five ums a minute free, never cut the breaths, same seat for thirty days, one device per episode, hide the join with sound) it is cited by section number, not rewritten.

Method: 27 WebSearch queries (extended for 2025 to 2026 material, standard for the record), one WebFetch attempt on Mosseri's December 31, 2025 Threads post, which failed (threads.com does not resolve from this container, so the memo is quoted through press coverage), and the repo's own breakdowns (Mariah, Jessica Carter, the account's 84 posts). Grades: **measured** (a study, a dataset, a platform's own statement of its policy), **practitioner** (a working creator, editor or vendor describing what they do or see), **folklore** (a number or rule repeated without a traceable origin). Where a figure comes only from a vendor blog it says so.

The short version. Nobody has published a skip rate for "looks AI". What exists is a consistent list of tells from people who detect synthetic video for a living, a set of 2026 surveys in which a third to two thirds of people say they engage or trust less once they can tell a thing is AI, and three platforms that in 2025 and 2026 wrote "mass-produced", "repetitive", "unoriginal" and "inauthentic" into their monetisation and recommendation rules. The human side has one platform statement (Mosseri, December 31, 2025: the signs of a human hand are what will be worth something in 2026), one old platform experiment (self-recorded beat studio 84 percent of the time in Stories, 2019), and a lot of practitioner evidence that lines up with the account's own 14.8 versus 1.4 percent. Sameness fatigue has no creator dataset at all; the usable evidence is sixty years of advertising wear-out and consumer satiation research, which says repetition hurts when the content is weak and the viewer stops attending, and that variety registered inside one named category is what slows the decline. Growth through people at 470 followers is mostly mechanics: Collabs put the post on both profiles, Live hosting is closed under 1,000, Reposts is the credited share, pods are a policy violation detected by pattern.

## 1. What makes a short video read as AI-made or template-made to a 2026 viewer

There are three layers, and viewers conflate them, so a video that is entirely human can read as slop if it wears the second layer's clothes.

**Layer one, generated media.** The detection people (Hany Farid via NPR, the Opus and SlopDetector guides) name: backgrounds rendered with less fidelity than the subject, the same patch repeating, on-screen text that degrades on a freeze frame, every subject lit the same way, oversaturated colour, lip-sync drift, objects that change count between shots, clips that are short because compute is expensive, action that starts and stops too cleanly, and a stock-sounding narration. One 2025 study in Perception is reported to have found viewers identified AI-generated motion from movement alone 83 percent of the time; the figure is secondhand. Farid's warning is that this layer is improving fast and even experts are fooled, so the tells are moving targets.

**Layer two, the template edit.** This is the layer that matters for Jacob, because his footage is real and his risk is the edit. No study names these tells; the evidence is an editor with thirteen years and 50K likes writing them on a notebook page (Mariah: "AI edits: overstimulating graphics, constant transitions, obnoxious sound effects, Cocomelon for grown-ups"), the account's own data (sponsor reels, the most templated thing it has made, skip at 72.5 percent against 32 for the best posts), and the library's cut-rate table (the sold AI-edit talking head lane sits in the bottom third on likes, doctrine 6.1). The named tells from the brief, graded: a synthetic or cloned voice (measured, see the voice studies below); word-by-word colour-flash captions, whoosh and riser SFX, a cut every 2 to 3 seconds on a clock, zoom punches on every join (practitioner, Mariah and the doctrine's own 131-reel table); stock or AI b-roll that illustrates the noun instead of adding information (practitioner, the one numeric b-roll test in doctrine 6.6); the over-lit, backdrop-and-key-light studio look (practitioner, now also platform guidance via the Mosseri memo); the same hook templates and the same thumbnail grid (practitioner, and YouTube's and Meta's "near-identical uploads" language); perfect grammar and no specific detail in the caption (folklore as a tell, but it is the cheapest to fix). The point that keeps surfacing: a human watching cannot tell whether a given cut was made by a person or a plug-in, so what they actually read is the rhythm. A clock rhythm reads as a machine whoever made it.

**Layer three, the account.** NPR and the detector guides say the account gives it away before the clip does: a profile that posts many near-identical items, no face, no place, no continuity, and comments full of people asking whether it is AI. YouTube's July 15, 2025 policy change renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content" and gave as its examples channels uploading nearly identical narrated stories or slideshows with repeated narration; in January 2026 it is reported to have terminated 16 channels with 35 million combined subscribers under it (a vendor figure, unverified). Meta's July 14, 2025 Facebook crackdown penalised more than 500,000 accounts for spam and fake engagement in the first half of 2025 (demoted comments, reduced distribution, no monetisation) and removed about 10 million profiles impersonating large creators; duplicate videos get reduced distribution so the original gets the views. Instagram's originality rule (April 30, 2024, extended to photos and carousels April 30, 2026) removes accounts that mostly post others' work from recommendations until most of a rolling 30 days is original, and says credit, a border, a watermark or captions do not make a copy original.

**How audiences say they react.** No survey measures a skip after an "AI info" label. The nearest proxies, all 2026 and all vendor-run: Power Digital (1,483 consumers) found 63 percent less likely to engage with a visual once they can tell it is AI; Clutch (601 consumers, August 2026) found 53 percent less likely to buy from a brand known to use AI in social content and nearly 90 percent wanting disclosure; Klaviyo and Datalily (8,000 consumers, eight countries) found 31 percent trust a brand less for using AI against 7 percent more; Meltwater and YouGov (about 10,000, seven markets, April 2026) found 86 percent want AI content disclosed; Sprout's Q1 2026 pulse found the top thing consumers want brands to stop doing is posting unlabeled AI content. Sprout's 2025 Index puts authenticity and relatability as the two traits consumers value most, with about half saying original content is what makes a favourite brand stand out; Gen Z values authenticity less (35 percent) than older groups, which matters for a 25 to 44 male audience.

**The voice, specifically.** The lab and the ad test disagree. A 2025 PLOS One study found listeners could not reliably tell voice clones from humans and rated generic synthetic voices as more trustworthy, not less. A January 2025 study from the Tilburg University repository found human voiceovers earned more trust in ads than AI voices, and Studio Resonate's August 2025 industry report put human voices 23 percent higher on trust with a 27 percent drop once listeners were primed to expect AI. Read together: a clone passes until the listener suspects, and suspicion is now the default on a feed. The one viewer quote that survives every blog: "It could be the most informative video, but I just HATE AI voices."

**What the platforms say about ranking.** Mosseri's year-end memo (Threads and a 20-slide Instagram post, December 31, 2025) opens "authenticity is becoming infinitely reproducible", lists "label AI-generated content and verify authentic content" and "continue to improve ranking for originality" as 2026 priorities, says labels are only part of the answer and that camera makers should sign media at capture. Press coverage (Nation Thailand, Stocktwits) adds that he expects attention to move to imperfect images, naming backlighting, shaky hands and small flaws as the signs of a human hand, and that success will hinge on uniqueness rather than production skill; those exact words are secondhand. In October 2026 Q&As he said Instagram does not rank AI creators differently and, asked whether it ranks AI content as well as other content, "probably not." The "AI info" label reads C2PA and IPTC metadata and invisible watermarks, not pixels; there is no published reach penalty on the post-level label; the account-level opt-in label was renamed "AI-generated profile" in August 2026 and Instagram says AI profiles that do not label may see reach limits. TikTok (November 18, 2025) added an AIGC slider in Manage Topics, invisible watermarks on AI content made with its tools or carrying C2PA credentials, and (guidelines effective September 24, 2026) requires creators to label realistic AI scenes or people; claims that unlabeled AI is downranked or removed have no primary source. None of this ranks a real person's handheld reel down; all of it ranks a template-farm account out of recommendations, and the "probably not" line says the viewer-side signals already do the rest.

## 2. What human-reading creators do differently, concretely

The measured base is thin and old, but it all points one way. Facebook's own 2019 Stories test found self-recorded creative beat studio-shot creative on content views 84 percent of the time. Motion's ad data (vendor, 2026) has lo-fi at 14 percent of creative assets and 42 percent of top performers. The one named creator case with numbers is Cava's lo-fi version at about 82,000 Instagram likes against about 15,000 for the polished one (one brand, one pair). The account's own ledger is the same shape: personal talking heads at 14.80 percent engagement against sponsor reels at 1.36 on the same reach. Against that, a December 2025 peer-reviewed study of 210 health videos found no correlation between content quality and engagement, which is the honest caveat: engagement does not track quality in either direction, it tracks whether a person is recognisably there.

Instagram's own statement is Mosseri's memo, and it is worth reading precisely. He did not say the algorithm now rewards raw footage; he said the signs of a human hand (backlight, a shaky hand, small flaws) are what becomes scarce when polish is free, and that the account that wins is the one whose output cannot be reproduced. That is a statement about the viewer, not the ranker. The "4.2x engagement for raw content" and "85 percent of Gen Z" figures circulating with it come from one blog citing unnamed research and should not be repeated.

What the human-reading creators actually do, from the breakdowns and the practitioner write-ups:

1. **They keep the interruption if it is real.** The doctrine already allows five ums a minute and forbids cutting breaths (4.4). Add: the coworker who walks through, the radio call, the dog, the wind gust, the line he restarts. One per episode, uncut, is a signature nobody can clone. Two is a mess.
2. **They say a specific thing.** Carter's 93-post audit: a small number beside the big one, the miss beside the win, "I'm not an expert" once. Keith Lee gives a score to one decimal and names the item. A caption with a date, a mileage and a part number cannot have been generated in bulk, and that is visible before the reel plays.
3. **They have one real set and it is theirs.** Mariah's desk with the green mug, Lee's driver's seat and the bag on his lap, Fisher's dark room, Carter's corner of the bed, Afualo's front camera. Doctrine 4.10 already fixes the seat; what the AI comparison adds is that the set must be lived in (the same coffee cup, the same dash clutter), because a studio backdrop is the one set a generator can also make.
4. **Their own voice, recorded in the room.** No clone, no text-to-speech, no voice-over recorded later in a quiet room over footage shot in a loud one. The DJI Mic stays on and the cab tone stays under. The voice studies say a clone passes until suspicion, and suspicion is now the default.
5. **Hand-written text instead of kinetic captions.** Mariah's eight notebook pages in 68 seconds is the whole case: the handwriting is proof of a hand, it changes the frame without a cut (doctrine 5.2), and it cannot be mistaken for a caption plug-in. Plain small captions are fine; word-by-word colour flashes are the tell.
6. **Real sound, and only real sound.** Doctrine 5.4 and 6.3. The addition here: no library SFX at all, because a whoosh is the single most recognisable template sound and it is free, so it appears on everything.
7. **Continuity across posts.** The same seat and the same first-sentence opener make the grid read as one person over time; NPR's tell for slop is the opposite, an account with no through-line. A numbered series ("day 14", "part one") is continuity the viewer can check.
8. **They answer in the same voice they film in.** Lee's advocacy for the family restaurants he reviews, Afualo's comment-section comedy, Carter's "ask the comments what to make next." The reply is the half of the video the viewer sees after; a reply written in the flat register of a generated caption undoes the reel.
9. **They re-film instead of re-templating.** Carter re-films her best video every 90 days with five takes per post. The human version of a repeat is a new take with the same idea, not the same file with a new caption.

What "raw" does not mean: bad audio, a face against the sun, or no preparation. Mariah is one locked shot with a podcast mic in frame. The imperfection that reads as human is in the delivery and the room; the sound is clean in every example above.

## 3. Sameness fatigue: does an identical daily series decay, and how do the long-runners vary it

No platform and no paper has published retention curves for a daily creator series with fixed framing. The blog literature on "format fatigue" is opinion, the "12 percent drop in session time" has no primary, and the one named case (The Comments Section ending daily uploads in May 2026 after a sustained engagement decline) is confounded by everything else about that show. So the question has to be answered from adjacent measured work plus what the long-running creators visibly do.

**The adjacent measured work says four things.** First, repetition does wear out, but through inattention and reactance rather than exposure count: Craig, Sternthal and Leavitt (1976) found recall wear-out only at very high repetition and it disappeared when attention was controlled; the ad-fatigue literature finds attention wear-out for static creative but not animated, faster wear-out for unfamiliar brands, and that design matters more than repetition count; Kantar's tracking found no creative wear-out across its diagnostics and blames weak creative and saturation when it appears. For a creator this reads: an unknown account (Jacob) wears out faster than a known one, and the thing that wears out is attention, which is exactly what a moving frame and a changing sentence restore. Second, the level at which the viewer files the episode decides how fast they tire of it (Redden 2008): people satiate less when they categorise each episode specifically ("the warranty story", "the day the F600 would not start") than generically ("another truck talking head"). The title line and the first sentence set that category. Third, and against intuition, framing the episodes as one named thing slows satiation rather than speeding it (Lasaleta and Redden 2018): when similar items are grouped as one category, the viewer registers the variety inside it. A named series with a fixed seat is therefore not the problem; a named series whose episodes are interchangeable is. Fourth, cadence interacts with variety (Galak, Kruger and Loewenstein 2011): the faster the consumption, the more variety the viewer wants; and recalling past variety restores appetite (Galak, Kruger and Redden 2009). A daily series needs more visible variation than a three-a-week one, and a callback to a different earlier episode is a cheap way to remind the viewer the series is not one thing.

**What the long-runners hold fixed and what they rotate.** Keith Lee: fixed car, bag on the lap, score per item, the family in the background; rotated restaurant, city (Atlanta, Houston, New York, Toronto as the first international stop), and whole runs with a different premise (the chain series "Who's Keeping Them Alive", a McDonald's "special edition"), and over years a shift from family-life content to reviews to advocacy. Jessica Carter: fixed corner of the bed and the flat opening claim; five or six rotating topics, the best video re-filmed every 90 days. Hank Green: fixed face thinking in real time; he told Threshold it was "weird to have too many episodes be about the circulatory system," which is the whole topic-rotation rule in one line, and he varied the vehicle (vlogbrothers, SciShow with rotating co-hosts and segments, the podcast at 502 episodes). Drew Afualo: fixed ritual open and the laugh; rotated target, and the long form moved to a podcast rather than stretching the reel. Jefferson Fisher: fixed dark set and the same framing; rotated scenario and the person being handled (from the repo's dossier and general knowledge, not re-verified this session). Mariah: fixed desk, mug and sweater; rotated the notebook page, the series number ("Part One") and the device (the list reel versus the notebook reel). The pattern across all six: identity constants are the set, the opening ritual and the voice; everything the viewer would call "the content" rotates, and the rotation is itself legible (a city, a part number, a named commenter) so the viewer files each episode under its own name.

**Is there A/B evidence for variation over consistency?** Not for creators. The nearest is Trial Reels data already in file 05 (two versions, one variable changed, judged at 24 hours), which is a tool for running the test, not a result. The account's own record is too short: three truck talking heads do not make a decay curve. The honest statement is that the decay of a daily identical series is predicted by the satiation literature, is consistent with what every long-runner does, and has not been measured on Reels by anyone who published.

## 4. Connecting to human growers at 500 followers

The mechanics, with what is and is not known about each:

**Collabs.** A Collab post is published once and shown on both profiles, to both sets of followers, with one shared pool of likes, comments and views that both authors see in Insights. Instagram makes no multiplier claim; the gain is the partner's audience minus the overlap, and for a 470-follower account partnered with a 5,000 to 50,000 pickup or diesel account the arithmetic is the largest single reach event available without ads. Mosseri named the Collab post (and the Repost button) in April 2026 as the sanctioned way to put someone else's work on your grid. The vendor figures ("30 to 50 percent more reach", "200 likes becomes 800") are folklore. Nobody has published small-account Collab data.

**Replying to every comment.** No controlled evidence that replies raise reach. Mosseri's position (October 2026, in file 05) is that comments are "not the most important part of value by a long shot"; the signals are watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach. The "two-way conversation signal since March 2026" is unsourced. Replies are for the relationship, for the pinned question that splits the comments, and because a reply in Jacob's voice is the second thing a new viewer reads about him. Reply to all of them while it is still possible to; that window closes somewhere past a few hundred comments a post, not at 470 followers.

**Commenting on larger accounts with substance.** No data at all; practitioner advice across every growth guide. The logic holds: a comment that adds a parts-counter fact under a 100K pickup creator's reel is read by that creator's audience in the hour the reel is hot, and it is the only free way to put his credential in front of them. Specific, no link, no pitch, in his own words.

**DM conversations.** Sends per reach is official and has been since spring 2024 (file 05); a DM conversation with someone who shared or replied is the human end of that signal. The API cannot start a DM (CLAUDE.md), the app can. This is the one channel where "connect to more human growers" means exactly what it says.

**Broadcast channels.** Professional account required; the follower threshold is not published and the guides disagree (100 to 10,000). No engagement data for small accounts. Low priority at 470; revisit at 1,000.

**Live with another creator.** Since August 2025 only public accounts with 1,000 or more followers can host a Live (TechCrunch, confirmed by Instagram). At 470 Jacob cannot host but can be a guest: a host can add up to three guests, and viewers of both accounts are notified. So "go Live with another creator" means being invited onto a 1K-plus peer's Live until the account crosses 1,000, which is one more reason the milestone matters.

**Crediting and the Reposts button.** Live since August 6, 2025: a repost goes to followers' feeds and a Reposts tab on the profile, credited, with all engagement staying on the original. Reposting a peer's reel with a line of his own in the comments is the legitimate share and costs the peer nothing. The opposite, a screenshot or re-upload with "credit" in the caption, is explicitly not original under the April 2026 rule, and an account that mostly reposts is removed from recommendations until most of its rolling 30 days is original again.

**Circles versus pods.** Meta's spam policy bans exchanging likes and follows and Instagram has said pods violate its rules; detection is behavioural (the same accounts engaging with each other, timestamps clustering right after posting, generic repeated comment text). No official source defines a legitimate circle, so the working test is this: a circle is five to ten peers who would watch and comment on each other anyway, whose comments carry a specific fact or a question, arrive when they actually watched, and would still be written if nobody reciprocated. The moment it becomes a schedule and a quota it is a pod, and it also stops working, because every ranking signal is a ratio and injected engagement pollutes the audience match that is this account's only asset (CLAUDE.md).

**Trial Reels** are not a relationship but they are the stranger-facing test, and file 05 puts the floor at 200 followers for a professional account (probable, verify in the composer), so the toggle should already be there.

## The AI tells, as a checklist

Each line: the tell, then the human alternative beside it.

- Cloned or text-to-speech voice. His own voice, recorded in the cab on the DJI Mic, with the cab under it.
- Voice-over recorded later over footage shot elsewhere. Say it where it was shot; if a line must be re-recorded, record it in the same seat with the engine state matched.
- Word-by-word colour-flash captions. Plain small captions two to four words a line, or the notebook page in his own hand.
- Whoosh, riser, pop or click on any join or text. Room tone under the cut, nothing else; at most one real sound from the cab or shop per reel.
- A cut every 2 to 3 seconds on a clock. Cut when the information changes; one or two removals in 60 s, on a breath or a gesture (doctrine 6.1, 6.2).
- A zoom punch on every cut. A camera move or a hand that changes the frame; a punch only on a locked shot and never with a cut (doctrine 6.3).
- Stock or generated b-roll that illustrates the noun. His own insert that adds a fact, one per idea, or none (doctrine 6.6).
- Key light, backdrop, ring light reflection in the eyes. Window light from the side in the real cab, the real clutter in frame.
- The same hook template every day ("Nobody tells you..."). The first sentence is the opener and it changes every episode; mid-action, a number, a quote, an object, a flat claim, rotated.
- The same cover frame with the same lockup placement on every tile. One lockup, but the frame behind it is a different place, prop or face each time.
- Perfect caption grammar, no date, no number, no name. A caption with the mileage, the part, the day, and one sentence he would say out loud.
- No breaths, no ums, no restarts. Breaths kept, up to five ums a minute, one real stumble (doctrine 4.4).
- Flat pitch, even pace, every sentence the same length. Pitch moves, the key sentence falls, one held beat after the turn (doctrine 4.2, 4.3).
- Nobody and nowhere: no place name, no coworker, no weather. Wyoming, the shop (with "I work there"), the cold, the person type from the counter, never a customer name.
- Posted in bulk, all alike, at the same minute. Posted when it happened, with the series number and a callback to a different earlier episode.
- Replies that read like the caption generator. Replies in the voice of the reel, specific to the comment, within the first hour.
- No trace of a hand: no handwriting, no fingerprints on the lens, no prop touched. The Sharpie on the pad, the part turned over in the hand, the page flipped on camera.

## Variation axes for a daily talking head

Constants for thirty days, per doctrine 4.10 and Jacob's rules: the seat as home base, his voice, the one lockup, no grade, no outro, no CTA, caption line 1 the share line. Everything below rotates, and the rotation should be visible in the title line so the viewer files each episode by name (section 3, Redden 2008).

| Axis | The options | How often to rotate |
|---|---|---|
| Opener type | Mid-action sentence; a number; a commenter's line read out; the object held up; a flat claim; a confession; a question he was asked at the counter | Every episode; never the same type two days running |
| Location | Truck cab (home base); the bay beside the F600; the parts room; the counter after hours; the driveway with Chief; walking the yard | Cab four days in seven; leave it at least twice a week |
| Device | Notebook page; the part in hand; the phone with the comment; a numbered card; nothing (funny episodes) | One device per episode (doctrine 5.2); no device two episodes in a row on the same subject |
| Length | 25 s (meme or one joke); 40 to 60 s (trust, default); 90 s (one story with a turn) | By job; at least one short and one long each week |
| Tone | Trust; funny; shared grievance held lightly; gratitude; low status; curiosity (thinking out loud) | Alternate; never two grievance episodes in a row |
| Subject lane | Relatable with any truck or none; Chief (the 6.7); the counter and Class 8; Rival check-in; craft (how I film this) | Roughly 2 relatable to 1 credential; Rival at most once a week until there are parts |
| Guest | None; a coworker's voice off camera; the owner's OK on camera once; a family member; a commenter by handle | Once a week at most; the one-man rule governs the time cost |
| Camera | Dash mount locked; ActiveTrack from the headrest; handheld walk; top-down on the notebook; passenger seat angle | Locked most days; one move episode a week |
| Light and time | Morning side light; after-hours shop fluorescents; dusk in the cab; headlights | Follows location; do not fix the posting hour to the frame |
| Payoff type | A number; a rule; a question for the comments; an open loop to a named next episode; a callback to an earlier episode | Rotate; an open loop at most twice a week |
| Format | Talking head; story told over footage; one-take walk; Q&A from comments; honest check-in | Talking head is the spine; one other format per week |
| Caption line 1 | A line someone would send to a friend; a number; a question; a quote from the reel | Every post; never the reel's first sentence repeated |
| Song | None; one quiet track inside Instagram | Mostly none; a song only on the montage or the meme |

The check that makes the editor challenge itself: before posting, list the last three episodes on five axes (opener, location, device, length, tone). If the new one matches more than two of the five on any single earlier episode, change one axis before it goes out. Once a month, re-film the best episode of the previous 90 days with a different opener and location (Carter) and compare watch share and likes per reach between the two.

## What it means for a one-man diesel creator

1. The frame must contain proof of a person: a hand, a page he wrote, the cab clutter, the Wyoming light. If a generator could have made the shot, change the shot.
2. His voice only, in the room it was shot in. No clone, no later voice-over, no library sound of any kind. The cab tone is the signature.
3. One true interruption per episode stays in. The coworker, the radio, the restart. Two is a mess; zero reads as a template over time.
4. Every caption carries one thing that could not be bulk-written: a mileage, a part number, a date, the day's temperature, a name he is allowed to use.
5. Cut on information, never on a clock; the sold AI-edit rhythm is the thing the 2026 viewer has learned to skip, and the account's own 72.5 percent sponsor skip rate is the local proof.
6. Hold the brand constants (seat, voice, lockup, no grade) and rotate the content axes openly, with the title line naming the episode so each one is filed on its own. A named series slows fatigue; interchangeable episodes speed it.
7. Run the five-axis check before every post and change one axis if the new episode matches more than two of five against any of the last three. Nothing should look the same, and the check is how that happens without a second person.
8. Re-film, never re-template: the best episode of each 90 days gets a new take with a different opener and location, not the same file with a new caption.
9. Replies are part of the edit. Answer every comment in the voice of the reel within the first hour while that is still possible, pin the question that splits the room, and take the best reply into a DM.
10. Spend the relationship effort where the mechanics pay: one Collab a month with a pickup or diesel account ten to a hundred times his size, substantive comments under three large accounts a week, a credited Repost of a peer's reel with a line of his own. No pods, no schedules of reciprocal likes.
11. He cannot host a Live until 1,000 followers; until then, be the guest. Trial Reels are open now and are the test surface for the opener rotation, two versions, one variable, judged at 24 hours.
12. Do not label his footage as AI and do not use anything that would trigger the label; and if a generated image or clip is ever used for a joke, say so in the caption in his own words, because the trust cost of being caught is larger than the reach cost of the label.
