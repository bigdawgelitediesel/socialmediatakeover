# One Peak Creative: the method, taken apart (2026-11-23)

Jacob, 2026-11-23: "Learn all you can about One Peak Creative and their creators. Go through those that have used their platform and formulate how they do what they do, so I don't have to pay for their program and can get similar results."

Built from public material only: One Peak's own sales pages, their YouTube videos (captions pulled with yt-dlp), two interviews with the founders, their free blog, Trustpilot, and the public TikTok and Instagram pages of the creators they name. Nothing from inside the paid course. Every claim has a source number in square brackets that points to section 8. Where I could not check something it says so.

Short version up front: the method is real but simple. One repeatable video structure per account, a first sentence that sets a stake or a question, the answer held to the last three seconds, and the same structure run over and over until it stops working. The rest of what they sell is feedback, a library of structures, and an AI app.

## 2. The method as they teach it in public

### 2.1 Watch time is the only number

Conar: "The only metric that mattered was watch time." [5] Their About page: "Higher watch time means higher views." [2] Everything below is in service of that.

### 2.2 The Four Keys (About page [2])

1. "The Hook Decides Everything". The first five seconds decide the video. They say to spend 80 percent of the effort there [2][7].
2. "Find Your Repeatable Format".
3. "Relatability is a Cheat Code".
4. "Focus on the Result".

### 2.3 The 3 Steps (the free masterclass [3])

1. **Find Your Winning Format.** "The 5 format categories every viral video falls into."
2. **Master the 5-Element Hook.** "The verbal, text, visual, audio, and caption hook system."
3. **Flip Into Viewer Mode.** "The pre-post audit that turns 'decent' videos into ones that pop. 5 extra minutes here can be the difference between 500 views and 5 million."

### 2.4 The five format categories, sorted by how they pay off [3][5]

| Category | The payoff at the end |
|---|---|
| Challenges | A win or a loss |
| Educational | The answer to the question the hook raised |
| Storytelling | The conflict gets resolved |
| Wait For It | A reaction (pranks, reveals) |
| Skits | An emotion, usually a laugh |

Conar on how the two ends connect: "The hook has to create an insane amount of tension and curiosity that is only resolved in the payoff." The payoff "has to land in the last three seconds." [5]

### 2.5 A format is a structure, not a topic

"The format is just the structure." [5] Their test for format versus trend: a format has to work in any niche and keep working over time [5]. Their pitch for any niche: "The framework is about structure, not topic." [4]

Named formats shown on their public pages [4][11]: Can You Tell the Difference?, Would You Rather?, The Blindfold Test, Quiz With Consequences, First vs Pro, Taste Test Challenge, So That's How They Do It, The Did You Know Format, The Personal Story Format, Expensive Secret Reveal (mundane item or mundane activity), the "classic reaction format", "unsolicited advice".

Sample hooks they show for those formats [4][11]:
- Can You Tell the Difference: "My partner swears he can tell a $3 sauce from a $40 restaurant one. Loser cooks dinner for a week."
- So That's How They Do It: "You've been searing scallops wrong your whole life. Here's the restaurant secret to a perfect crust."
- Personal Story: "I got fired on my 30th birthday. Here's what happened next."
- Expensive Secret Reveal: "My husband just spent $500 on our kitchen, and you won't believe what he actually bought."

### 2.6 Repeat one format until it quits

Conar on Gerry (Meg's dad, the golf caddy): "He has created an entire career off of this one repeatable video idea." [5] Their examples of people outside the program doing the same thing: MrBeast, Zach King, Caleb Simpson, Alix Earle [2][5]. Their own podcast keeps coming back to it: "keep the meter running", "will it blend", "what's inside". Each is one sentence you can film forever [14].

### 2.7 The hook

- Five elements: verbal (first spoken line), text (words on screen), visual (what is in frame), audio, caption [3]. An affiliate reviewer of the Art of Hooks course lists five different words (clarity, intrigue, emotion, urgency, value) [15]. I trust the masterclass page since it is theirs and current. The paid course may use either list. Unverified which.
- Curiosity over a bold claim. Meg: a bold statement to camera is not enough [7]. Her own example: a trip video flopped with a plain "come with us" open, then did about 2 million when the open became a curiosity line about her daughter being scared of sand [7].
- **Personal experience beats generic.** Their blog: "3 Tips For New Dog Owners" becomes "My puppy's first day at home was a disaster, BUT I learned 3 very important things in the process…" [16] Templates from the same post: "I tested ___ so you don't have to. Here's my story." "I spent $___ on ___. Was it worth it?" "The real cost of ___. 2 things I wish I knew earlier..." [16]
- **The curiosity gap.** Give "just enough information to intrigue a viewer" and hold the rest [17]. Open with a teaser, never the punchline. Add a twist. Ask a question that gets answered at the end.
- **The secondary hook.** From their podcast: the first hook gets the click ("if you go on a 12-hour road trip with me I'll pay your first payment"), the second one keeps people to the end ("you're not going to know where we're going till the end"). Glen: "making sure that there's a reason to watch to the end." [14]
- The visual hook starts with where you stand: pick a spot that stands out while people scroll [7]. Their iPhone-versus-cinema-camera test: 96,000 views against 125,000, "the gear actually has nothing to do with the success of a video." They also changed location several times in one short video "because we really want to keep people's attention." [18]
- The curiosity test for a hook: "If this makes you curious, it'll make your audience curious." [5]

### 2.8 A stake in the first sentence

This one is not named on their pages but it is in every challenge video they and their students make: "if X happens, I do Y." Gerry buys lunch or a golf bag. Glen does a month of laundry. Loser cleans the house [5][19]. The stake is what turns a demo into a question with an answer.

### 2.9 Value and relatability

Meg: "lead with entertainment", then the value, then a soft sell [7]. "You have 5 seconds to convince viewers that YOUR video is going to be better than what's coming next." [6] From the camera test: it "all comes down to the story, the relatability, the shareability, the characters." [18] From the podcast: "either give a ton of value or absolutely no value at all." [14]

### 2.10 Strangers and other people on camera

Their podcast calls stranger content "low hanging fruit": "you can never get sick of stranger content... always a new person, always a new conversation." They call skits "one of the harder things to go viral" with [14].

### 2.11 Posting cadence

Fewer than you would think. Blog: about 3 a week while you are testing formats; once you have a winner, whatever you can keep up, "a slam-dunk video once a week, that's totally cool!" and "You should never post just for the sake of posting." [20] Meg on the podcast: one to three strong pieces a week beats daily [7]. The guarantee only asks for one post a week [4].

### 2.12 Calls to action

They teach one on every video: spoken in the last 2 seconds, or a text block, plus the caption right after the caption hook. Keep it to 2 or 3 seconds or people scroll. Examples: "Tag someone you know who NEEDS to see this!", "Comment 'reserve my seat' and we'll DM you the link", ManyChat for the auto-DM [21]. Their own analyzer screenshot flags "Don't tell viewers to scroll away" and says to end on a question or a curiosity gap [4].

### 2.13 Scripts, editing, AI

- Script shape in their app: hook about 10 s, body about 34 s, CTA about 5 s, in 15, 30, 45 or 60 second sizes [1].
- Editing: cut dead air, burn in captions, keep the pace. That is what the one-click editor does [4][11]. The $58 course has 10 editing lessons (transitions, shortcuts) [8].
- AI: the app writes hooks and scripts. Conar says to learn "AI-assisted" content, not "AI-generated" [5]. The $58 course now has a lesson on making content with VEO3 (AI video) [8], and their Honest Ads channel posted an AI-made parody in 2025 [19].

### 2.14 Topic picking

Three to five content pillars. Pick them from your goals, what the audience needs, and a keyword search inside the apps. Then write hooks under each pillar and film the ones you like most [22]. In the app: type in your niche, pick a format, get hooks [4].

### 2.15 Money

In this order: views first so brands want you, then brand deals, affiliates (they sell an Amazon affiliate course), then your own product or service [4][1]. Conar's golf video: post 10 to 20 videos, then pitch local businesses with the account as proof, start small (free tee time), give them a free sample video first [23]. One member in a service business says the wins came from collecting emails: "Sales happen in emails more than in DMs." [4]

### The shared pattern (this is the method)

1. **One structure per account, and the first sentence is nearly word for word the same every time.** Gerry's caddy ask. "Did y'all know if you take." "Your manager won't admit this." "Let's prove them wrong." Viewers know what they are getting in two seconds.
2. **The first sentence holds a stake, a number, or a secret.** A bet with a loser. A dollar figure. Something an insider knows. Never "today I'm going to show you".
3. **The answer is the last thing in the video, and then it stops.** The putt, the taste, the reveal, the finished tattoo. No recap.
4. **A person and a personal angle, not a topic.** "My wife", "my client", "my husband", "a senior leader told me". Relatable first, expert second.
5. **The caption teases the ending, it does not explain the video.** "Her face on the last one.." Then a question to the comments.
6. **Length follows the format.** One line of inside knowledge is 13 s. A bet with a result is about 60 s. A full hole of golf is 3 minutes. Nobody pads.
7. **Plain production.** Phone, daylight, burned-in captions, no fancy graphics. Nothing I saw would take more than an hour to edit.
8. **They ask.** Nearly every one ends on a spoken "let me know in the comments" or "hit follow for episode three".
9. **Money is built into the format.** Gerry's prize is the sponsor's product. Becca's updates carry the ad. The brand is the stake, not a break in the video.
10. **Most posts are ordinary.** Even the showcase creators sit at a few thousand views on a normal post and get a big one maybe one time in five to ten. The format does not make every post hit. It makes the hits repeatable when they come.

## 5. The do-it-yourself version for @elcoyotediesel

This is built to sit on top of what is already decided: two talking heads a day (`playbook/13-two-a-day-30-day-plan.md`), one Trial Reel a day (`research/2026-11-23-TRIAL-REELS-PLAYBOOK.md` and `-SCHEDULE.md`), community first, relatable over build talk. Nothing here adds a fourth post. It changes how the three get written and read.

### Step 1. Pick topics the One Peak way, from the bank you already have

For every idea, write one line: **the question a stranger wants answered, and the last shot that answers it.** If you cannot name the last shot, it is not a video yet. Then tag the idea with one of the five categories:

| Category | What it looks like here | One-man? |
|---|---|---|
| Challenge | A bet against the truck, the clock or yourself | Yes |
| Educational | One thing the counter taught you, answer at the end | Yes |
| Storytelling | First truck, hotshot nights, the wrong part | Yes |
| Wait For It | First start, first crank in the cold, the thing under the tarp | Yes |
| Skit | The existing meme and skit bank | Mostly |

The sixty talking heads already carry "the first sentence, the turn, the last line". This step only adds the category tag so the Sunday read can say which category earns.

### Step 2. Write the first sentence from a formula

Six formulas, each one lifted from a One Peak format and reworded. Brackets are your real numbers. Do not film until the bracket is true.

| Formula | Their version | Yours |
|---|---|---|
| A. Stake | "If we can make par, I'm taking her to get anything she wants" | "If X happens, I do Y" |
| B. Insider secret | "Your manager won't admit this, but" | "The parts counter won't tell you this, but" |
| C. Personal story | "I got fired on my 30th birthday" | "I [did the dumb thing]. Here's what it cost" |
| D. They said no | "10 artists told my client this wasn't possible" | "[Who] told me this couldn't be done" |
| E. Can you tell | "She says she's a water snob" | "Can a parts guy tell [A] from [B]" |
| F. Real cost | "I spent $___ on ___. Was it worth it?" | Same, your number |

Example hooks. First spoken sentence, in your voice, to be re-said your way:

1. "I order parts for a living, and I still ordered the wrong one for my own truck." (C)
2. "The parts counter won't tell you this, but the cheap one is the one I sell twice." (B)
3. "If this '59 F600 doesn't fire in three tries at [temp] degrees, I'm [the thing you don't want to do]." (A, Wait For It)
4. "My first truck cost me $[x]. The dumbest thing I did to it cost more than the truck." (C, F)
5. "I've got [N] months to get a 1959 F600 to the SEMA floor. Right now it runs and it can't move." (A as a running story, the Becca play)
6. "[N] people have told me a 7.3 doesn't belong in a '59 F600. Let's find out who's right." (D, only if the count is real)
7. "Can a parts manager tell a $[x] filter from a $[y] one with his eyes closed?" (E, two parts in a bag, one man)
8. "I spent $[x] on [part] for my 6.7. Was it worth it? Tell you at the end." (F)
9. "Every customer says 'it just started doing that.' Here's what that sentence usually costs." (B)
10. "I drove hotshot for [N] years. One night I almost quit, and what stopped me wasn't money." (C)
11. "Three things I would never buy used, from the guy who sees what comes back." (B)
12. "A 7.3 guy and a 6.7 guy walk up to my counter. I can tell which is which before they talk." (B, funny)
13. "This truck sat for [N] years. Today I find out if the [thing] still works." (Wait For It)
14. "I told my wife what this build would cost. Here's the number I didn't say out loud." (Expensive Secret Reveal, only with a true number)

Check each against their curiosity test: read it cold and ask if you would stay to find out. If not, rewrite.

### Step 3. Build the five-element hook, inside the house rules

| Their element | House-rule version |
|---|---|
| Verbal | The formula sentence above. It is the first sound. No "hey guys" |
| Text | The one lockup, 3 to 7 words, a number if there is one, gone by 3 s. Already the rule |
| Visual | Start with the thing in frame: the part in hand, the truck that won't move, the cold start. Pick the spot that stands out, not the same seat every time |
| Audio | Your voice in the room. Song added in-app after |
| Caption | Line 1 is the share line. Write it so it also points at the ending (see conflict 5) |

### Step 4. Hold the answer to the last three seconds and cut

Promise it in sentence one, pay it in the last line, end on the payoff frame. This is already "no outro, no fade". Where the story has a middle sag, add the secondary hook from their podcast: a second small question raised around the one-third mark ("and that's before I found out what was under the seat").

### Step 5. Find the repeatable format with a fixed test

One Peak gives members about 8 weeks to test several formats and keep one. Your version, using slots you already have:

- **Weeks 1 and 2 (Nov 24 to Dec 7):** run the calendar as written. Tag every post with its category and formula letter in the ledger.
- **Dec 7 or 8 read (already scheduled):** rank by sends per reach. Name the top two as formats in one sentence each, the way they do ("guy at the counter tells you the one thing he would never buy").
- **Weeks 3 and 4:** give the winner one fixed slot three days a week. Same first-sentence shape, same length, new subject each time. Change one thing per post. That is their "repeat" and your one-variable rule doing the same job.
- **Trial Reels are the lab.** HOOK TEST days already exist on Thursday and Sunday. Use them to run formula against formula on the same story (say A against C). Lower skip rate wins and goes on the main-feed post.
- **Stop rule:** a format that has run six times with sends per reach under the account median is done. Pull the next one.

### Step 6. The viewer-mode check, five minutes before posting

One Peak does not publish their checklist. This is mine, built from what they say in public:

1. Phone in hand, sound off. Does the first frame plus the lockup tell a stranger what the question is?
2. Sound on. Is the first spoken sentence the hook, with nothing in front of it?
3. At 5 seconds, do you know what you are waiting for?
4. Is there any stretch over about 4 seconds where nothing new happens? Trim it.
5. Does the answer land in the last 3 seconds, and does the video end right there?
6. Would you send this to one person you can name? If you cannot name him, it is not a send episode.

### Step 7. Weekly rhythm

No new filming days. The sit at 16:30 and the edit at 20:00 stay.

| When | What changes |
|---|---|
| Daily sit | Say the formula sentence three ways before the first take. Keep the best open |
| Daily edit | Run the six-point viewer check after `streamline.py lint` |
| Thu and Sun trial | Formula A versus formula B on the same story |
| Sunday read | Add two columns: category, formula. Rank by sends per reach. Write the top format as one sentence |
| Every other Sunday | Decide: repeat the winner, retire the loser |

### Step 8. What to measure

Keep your grades: sends per reach and 7-day views for send episodes, watch share and likes per reach for the hold episodes, skip rate under 40. Add from One Peak:
- Watch share on every post as the check on whether the payoff is held late enough. If watch share is low and skip rate is fine, the middle is the problem, not the hook.
- Hits per ten, by format. Their best creators hit about one in five to ten. A format that hits one in ten and can be filmed any day is a keeper.

### Step 9. Money, the Gerry way

When a partner comes on, do not make a sponsor reel. Make the partner's product the stake or the answer in a format that already works. Gerry's prize is the sponsor's golf bag, and it is the reason to watch, not an ad break. That fits the Rival partner approach and it fixes the account's old problem (sponsor posts at 1.36 percent engagement against 14.8 for personal).

## 6. What the paid program gives that this cannot

Straight answer.

**What you would be buying for $5,999:**
1. **Someone looking at your draft before it posts.** A coach writes back the one fix, usually the hook or the pacing. This is the real product. You cannot fully do this for yourself, because you already know what the video is about and a stranger does not.
2. **Two new formats a week, explained.** A library of 50-plus structures with examples. Saves you the time of watching other people's hits and taking them apart.
3. **A weekly call and a group.** Accountability, and people at the same stage to compare notes with.
4. **A monthly money session.** Brand deal rates, how to negotiate, affiliates.
5. **The app.** Ideas, auto-edit, a retention read-out.

**Does the gap matter for you?**
- Draft review: partly covered. `streamline.py challenge` checks each cut against the doctrine, and Trial Reels give you the read from actual strangers in 24 hours, which is better evidence than a coach's opinion, only slower. What is missing is a human outside eye. A cheap fix: send the first five seconds to one person who is not a truck guy and ask what the video is about. If they cannot say, redo the open.
- Format library: mostly covered. You have a studied-reel library, the outlier matrix, and now the named formats in section 2.5. The names plus one example each are enough to film from.
- Accountability: you are posting three times a day on a written calendar. This is not your problem.
- Money: you already have live partner talks (ISSPRO, Power Hungry, BD, CNC Fab, Strictly Diesel). A general brand-deal workshop is less useful than those real conversations.
- The app: against your rules (AI scripts and AI edit), and you already have an edit pipeline.

**So:** at 577 followers, with conversion as the known bottleneck, $6,000 buys mostly things you have or have ruled out. The one thing worth money is the outside eye, and you can get that for free from a person and from Trial Reels. If you ever do want to look inside without the big bill, the free masterclass (Tue Nov 24, 11am Mountain) costs 90 minutes and ends in a sales pitch, and the $29 a month app tier includes the format walkthroughs. I would not buy either before the Dec 8 read. If you buy anything from them, read the checkout page for the word "monthly" first (section 4).
