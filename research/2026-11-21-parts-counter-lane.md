## 0. The finding

**The lane really is empty, and the one person who proves it can work is not a parts guy.** Every account that makes parts-counter content at scale is either a skit account played by a service-advisor type (dealerplateguy, 143K TikTok, 130K Instagram), a tool-truck dealer (Val Tools, 254.7K TikTok, 170K YouTube), or a supplier posting a catalog with a face in front of it (Highway and Heavy Parts, 11.5K followers, 100 to 400 plays per clip). The only actual counter people found are a John Deere parts salesman in Nebraska (jordyjdpartsguy, 4,513 followers) and a Mopar parts guy whose account went quiet in 2024 (henikcuf). No heavy-duty dealership or distributor parts person has a working creator account; FleetPride and TruckPro have no TikTok videos at all; the only "day in the life of a heavy-duty parts man" on YouTube (Transwest Trucks) has 830 views.

**What the measured clips say about why.** The sends go to the clips that put a person on the other side of the counter and let the viewer recognize them: the new hire told to "just say parts" (1.24 percent), the counter guy asking irrelevant questions for wiper blades (1.68 percent), the tech melting down while the parts guy stays on the phone (1.63 percent), the parts salesman's six-second answer to "advice for someone starting at the parts counter?" (2.90 percent). The clips that put a part in frame with no person attached do not send, however correct the number: HHP's "the $50 part protecting your $40k engine" got 395 plays and zero shares, their "cheapest part on the shelf might cost you more later" got 103 plays. And the biggest parts clip of the year, dealerplateguy's 1.5M-view core-charge sketch, sent at 0.01 percent: people laughed at the confused customer and sent it to nobody, because the sketch mocks the person who would receive it.

So the lane for Jacob is not "the parts guy explains" and not "the parts guy does skits." It is **the part in his hand, one number, and the name of the person who needs to hear it**, said by someone who visibly works the counter. That is the gap between HHP's 0 percent and jordy's 2.9 percent, and nobody in heavy duty is standing in it.

**The send triggers in this tribe, from what measured:**

1. **The exact bill.** "$3,408.94 repair bill" on a 5.3K-sub channel did 18,922; "The real cost of truck breakdowns" on a 250K channel did 3,766. Owner-ops send the receipt, not the lesson. The cents matter because they prove it is a real invoice.

3. **Engine identity.** X15 vs MX-13 vs DD15, C15 nostalgia, "3 semi trucks that never break down." The PeterbiltX channel exists entirely on "X vs Y, the real repair cost will shock you" titles. An owner-op will send a part reel to another owner-op only if it names his engine.
4. **The small thing that took the truck down.** The $9 clip, the DEF head, the NOx sensor, the coolant sensor, the OneBox ("Freightliner OneBox failure could cost you $17,000"). The tribe's recurring story is a cheap part, a long wait, a big number.
5. **"Should have known."** Maintenance-reserve content ("the $0.12 a mile reserve," "how much to save for maintenance") is advice, and advice gets saved, not sent. It becomes a send when it is told as a specific truck that did not have the reserve.

**Can a parts manager reach this tribe without a mechanic on camera? Yes, with two conditions.** The evidence is Val Tools (sells to mechanics, never shows a repair, 254.7K) and jordy (sells parts to farmers, never shows a field repair, 2.9 percent sends on the counter clips). The conditions: (a) the credential has to be visible in every clip, which at the counter means the bins, the screen, the phone, the boxes, the shop floor behind the glass, the part in hand; a parts manager explaining from his truck cab reads as a pundit; (b) the tribe has to be addressed by name, because an owner-op does not send a reel to "truckers," he sends it to the one guy with the same engine or the same bill. The mechanic is not required. What is required is the thing a mechanic cannot show: the returns, the backorder screen, the core tag, the two boxes side by side, the count ("fourteen of these this month").

**Where the semi content sits in his week.** Per CLAUDE.md, one semi post in three. This tribe converts to shares and to credibility with the pickup audience, not to followers at first; owner-ops follow slowly and send fast. Measure it on sends per reach, not follows.

## 4. Guardrails: what a parts manager can show from the counter

Read today: the FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ (ftc.gov, full text) and the NLRB's social media page. The 2026 "creators who got in trouble" examples the brief asked for could not be searched this session (budget exhausted); the precedents below are from memory and marked. Jacob should treat this as the rulebook and have Troy or Holly read it once, since the shop's exposure is theirs.

**The FTC side (this is the one with teeth for a sponsored creator).**
- An employee who posts about products his employer sells is an endorser, and "I work there" has to be in the video, not in the bio. The FTC's words: listing the employer on the profile does not count because viewers of a single post never see it; "#employee" is not clear enough; "my employer's" or "I work for XYZ" in the body of the message is the clearest form. For Jacob that means the counter being visible is not a disclosure; one spoken line ("I run the parts counter at a semi shop") in any clip that recommends a part the shop sells is.
- Free product requires disclosure even if it is returned; an ongoing brand relationship requires disclosure in each new post; the disclosure goes at the start or right before the endorsement, in the video, visual for visual claims and audio for audio ones; on TikTok, in large superimposed text rather than the description. Short labels "Ad:" or "#ad" at the start of the caption are acceptable; "gifted" and "thanks to" are not.
- **Comparative claims while sponsored:** if he criticizes a competitor of a brand that pays or supplies him, the FTC says the paid relationship must be disclosed because it changes the weight of the criticism. FASS is a relationship. A "don't buy this lift pump" reel about a FASS competitor is the single most exposed clip he could make. The CP4-disable story is safe because it is his own truck and his own receipt; the comparison reel is not.
- Honesty: he cannot describe an experience he did not have or call mediocre good. Return-rate claims are statements of fact and must be true as stated.

**The NLRB side (does it protect him from being fired for a post?).** Only if the post is concerted: pay, conditions, a group complaint. A solo gripe about the job, the boss, a vendor or a customer is not protected. Wyoming is at-will. Nothing in this lane is protected speech; everything in it runs on the owners' goodwill, which is another reason the credential is allowed to be visible (the owners know) and the shop name stays out of the frame on content they did not approve.

**What he can show from the counter, by object:**

| Object | Show | Do not show |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice or repair order | A crop of one line: part description, quantity, a round price. Or a prop reprint. | Customer name, company name, unit number, VIN, plate, RO number, tech name, phone, address, the shop header, dollar totals that could be matched to a real customer. Fullbay screens never on camera. |
| Vendor pricing | List or retail price, round numbers, "about." What he paid on his own truck, to the cent. | Dealer cost, jobber cost, vendor cost sheets, the vendor's name next to a cost, warranty or rebate terms. Supplier agreements routinely make cost confidential and the shop's accounts are not his to risk. |
| The returns sheet | The sheet with every name column covered, the part in hand, a count ("three of nine this quarter at my counter"). | Vendor names tied to the count unless he has the log and the owners agree; customer names; photos of the full tracker. |
| Brand verdicts | Documented experience, with the sample size and the time window spoken: "at my counter, this year, three of these came back with the same crack."
