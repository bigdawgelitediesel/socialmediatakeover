## 4. Philipp Humm, storytelling in five techniques (the one to apply first)

A storytelling coach's nine minutes, 2.3M views. Thesis in one line: "The best stories don't just summarize events. They don't stay at that helicopter level. They zoom into the moment." He opens with John Krasinski's 20-second customs-agent story ("you married Emily Blunt?") to show a moment told from inside, then gives five techniques, each with a before and after.

1. **Location. Say where you are.** "Two weeks ago I'm sitting on my couch in my living room." One location, no details: "they have their own version of that location."
2. **Actions. State the verbs.** "I open my laptop and start reading a message by my manager." Actions give forward momentum and tell the listener you will not waste their time.
3. **Thoughts. Share the raw ones.** Not "I was excited to meet my crush" but "I thought, ah, this will be so cool, finally." Not "this represents a supreme opportunity" but "the raw, unfiltered, a little bit neurotic thoughts. That will make your story much more relatable."
4. **Emotions. Show them on the body.** Not "I was relieved" but "I lean backward and let out this big sigh." Not "he was anxious" but "he kept tapping his pen on the table and glancing at the clock."
5. **Dialogue. The exact words.** "My friend looked at me and said, Philipp, what on earth was that?" Dialogue is "the tool that I use the most." Make it juicy and short, never "I'm very dissatisfied with the inadequate execution of that project."

He closes with Sarah Willingham's one-minute story (the lawyer who assumed she was the coffee girl: "mine's white with one sugar please"; she made the coffee, sat back down, and "watched the colour drain from his face"), which carries all five.

**Apply.** This is the missing craft layer under playbook/12. Every talking-head script now gets the five-technique pass before filming: where was I (the counter, the bay, Chief's cab at 6 a.m.), what was I doing (the verb), what did I actually think (the rude version), what did it look like on me (the body, not the adjective), what did the other person say word for word. The Harley Trade reel already had technique five by accident ("how much you pay for that truck?") and it held at 38 percent skip. The gratitude footage has none of the five, which is why it feels general; the recut cannot add them, the next shoot can. Rule for the script file: at least three of the five in the first 20 s.

## 3. Ronny Mitchell, the cult-like personal brand (six characteristics)

Sixteen minutes, 107K views, a creative director who says he has produced ten million followers for clients. Opening line: "You don't just want followers. You want followers that would notice if you disappeared." Six characteristics.

1. **Dogma.** "Your core belief, the thing you would bet your reputation on." Get it by dictating your life story to an AI and having it interview you for the beliefs. "Sprinkle in your dogma so that people that look at the world the way you look at it follow you." His example: a business coach grown to 2M followers "strictly by getting him to talk about his beliefs" on parenting, relationships, money. "People do business with people they like."
2. **Credibility.** "Third-party demonstrated proof that you are who you say you are." His blunt example: a fitness coach who is "wrong about everything" but has mountains of client transformations, so the audience trusts him anyway. Liking is not enough to be influenced; proof is.
3. **Personality.** "The traits, the isms, the behaviours." The creator with the "big boy voice" who was nothing like himself on camera: "Brother, I don't like you. Maybe this is why you're not growing." Create content that gets the best moments of yourself out; do not create a persona you will feel trapped in. "On social media you will grow for not fitting in."
4. **Counter-positioning.** The law of contrast (one red circle on white paper). Choose what you stand for and what you stand against, and do it in more than one dimension: not only in your industry but in how you think about work, money, fitness, family. "The number one trait of a successful creator is multi-dimensionalism."
5. **A current goal, documented publicly.** A creator at 400K who went to a million with a daily challenge video. "A leader is someone who leads someone somewhere." Setting a goal puts you back in the student seat and builds credibility while you show the work. His example is Sam Gaudet himself: documenting 100K followers with an iPhone.
6. **Outside interests.** "Credibility porn": when someone is good at one thing, people want their take on everything. Share the interests outside the niche so the audience sees "you breathe the same air."

He ends by saying several principles can live in one video.

**Apply.** Jacob already has 5 by accident: Project Rival, Road to SEMA 2027, is the current public goal, and the daily talking head is the documentation. The two missing are 1 and 4 said out loud. Dogma: a one-page list of what Jacob believes about trucks, work and money that every episode can lean on (candidates from the sessions: the project truck is the one you already own; most of what gets bought at my counter was never needed; parts is a trade; a FASS is not insurance, it does both jobs). Counter-positioning in more than one lane: not a mechanic, a parts manager; not a shop owner, works at one; not a 750 hp build, a truck that runs badly and does not move. Personality: the house rule that his voice ships and the scratch voice never does is this principle. Outside interests: the gratitude post and the porch are this, and the community rule already protects them.

## 5. Kallaway, how the algorithms actually work (the matchmaker)

Seventeen minutes, 1.33M views. The model: "The algorithm is just one giant matchmaker." When a video is posted the platform builds a "digital fingerprint" from vision, audio and metadata, maps the topic, builds a fit score (who will like this), and shows it to "roughly 200 people" first, mostly non-followers, because "it wants to test how well strangers react." Positive data: 2,000, then 20,000, then 200,000, until the data weakens. Neutral: resample another 200. Negative: stop. "If you post a video and it flops, the algorithm got bad data back from that initial sample group of 200."

Two levers only. First, help it build a better fit score: "consistently make videos about the same topic for the same audience avatar over and over." He calls this audience matching and says it is the one principle he swears by. Mixed topics give mixed samples and the next video flops: "even one viral hit to an audience outside of your core demo will result in the next several videos having poor sample data." Second, make the sample engage. The three metrics he names: average watch time (and completion), engagement rate (likes plus comments plus shares over views), and "watch time session share", which nobody can see. The four things that raise them: the topic is relevant to the ideal viewer; the information is non-obvious and implementable; the viewer can absorb it; there is a short distance to implement. "Cover a core pain point, have something useful to say, say it so they understand, and they can apply it on their own."

Comments bonus: take a hard stance; take the contrarian side; ratchet the framing up; build topics around cult-loved brands and ideas; drive emotion. Closing: "Posting time does not matter. Hashtags don't matter. The captions themselves don't matter. The only thing that matters is making great videos for a specific avatar group across a narrow band of topics over and over. That is the cake."

**Apply, and one caution.** The audience-matching rule is the strongest argument in the five for the two-lane plan (pickup audience, parts-counter credential) and against the drift of November: thank-you reels, giveaways and partner shout-outs sampled to the wrong 200 and the next posts paid for it (08-our-own-work.md, question 37: product, thank-you and ask openers lose two thirds of viewers in three seconds). The four horsemen are a script test for every trust episode. The caution: his "captions don't matter" is about ranking, not about sends; the account's own data says the caption's first line is where the send reason lives, and Instagram has named sends per reach as a signal three years running. Keep both. His comment tactics are the ones the house uses sparingly (one contrarian take per episode, said flat), not ratcheted framing for its own sake.

## 2. Jake Trinder, the YouTube training (demand first, then packaging, then satisfaction)

Thirty-six minutes, a recorded agency training by a 22-year-old who runs over a hundred business YouTube channels (Gary Vee's among them). It is about long-form YouTube and business funnels, so most of it does not transfer directly; five ideas do.

1. **Evidence of demand before you press record.** "Knowing your audience matters, but it does not prove that they will choose the subject on YouTube." His depth-first search: seed keywords from an AI, search each, open every outlier video, open its channel, record every outlier, follow recommendations, back up, next term, until the whole niche is mapped. "If you only research winners, you can prove any bad idea": study the failures of a format too.
2. **Levels of awareness (Breakthrough Advertising).** The insulin doctor wanted to talk about insulin; her audience wanted fat loss. "If you don't understand insulin, you don't understand fat loss": position the unfamiliar mechanism around the outcome they already want.
3. **Packaging: do not copy the words, understand why people clicked.** "How to lose visceral fat so fast it feels like cheating" works because the audience wanted it gone, fast, unfairly. A format is the psychology, not the sentence.
4. **The open: promise, proof, plan.** "Am I getting what I clicked for? Can this person actually deliver it? Is the rest worth my time?" Reaffirm the promise, give the unfair edge in one sentence ("nail the statement so you can use it in every video"), show where the video is going.
5. **Viewer satisfaction is the goal, not retention.** "Editing can remove friction from the process, but it cannot rescue a weak idea or empty video." "What most editing gurus call retention is just decoration. If a visual does not clarify, prove, or help the viewer follow the video, it's just movement and you don't need it." Also: "Specificity always charges you in reach. Pay that price when the right person feels more understood." And on scripting: "A full script can make a strong expert worse on camera"; script tightly only where the copy must be tight.

On the ask: "Instead of a take, a give." The call to action is the most useful next step (a creative library, a scorecard), never "value, value, value, run and pitch."

**Apply.** The demand-first method is the Outlier Matrix method already in the repo, with one addition: map the failures of a format as well as the winners before calling it a rule. The levels-of-awareness idea is the FASS lesson restated: nobody searches "lift pump", everyone wants the truck to start every morning and make power later, so the episode is titled on the outcome and the mechanism is the surprise. Promise, proof, plan is the first ten seconds of any list episode. "Just movement" is the sentence to pin over the edit bay; it is Mariah's thesis from the opposite end of the business.

## What the five say together

- Humm gives the sentence-level craft (zoom into the moment), Ronny the identity layer (dogma, proof, personality, contrast, a public goal), Kallaway the distribution physics (one avatar, narrow topics, four horsemen), Trinder the decision discipline (evidence of demand, packaging psychology, satisfaction over retention), Caleb the brand system over all of it.
- All five are long talking heads with few cuts and a visible structure (numbered techniques, six characteristics, two levers, five steps). The form of the lesson is the lesson: a person, a seat, a list, change by card or page, and a hard stop.
- Three of the five say some version of "editing is decoration if the idea is empty" (Trinder outright, Kallaway's "that is the cake", Humm by teaching only words). It is the doctrine's spine.
