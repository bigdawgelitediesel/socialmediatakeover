# 03. The hook as a human, and spoken delivery (questions 13 to 21)

Researched 2026-11-22. Method: WebSearch only (about 40 queries, standard then extended). WebFetch failed on every host tried (emplifi.io, journals.sagepub.com), so figures come from search snippets and secondary coverage, and the page-level numbers should be re-checked before anyone quotes them outside this repo. Each source is graded: **measured** (a dataset or an experiment), **practitioner** (a working creator, coach or vendor saying what they do), **folklore** (repeated with no origin). Where I lean on a paper I know but could not re-verify today, I say so.

One honest finding up front: there is **no public retention dataset for talking-head Reels specifically**. The best measured work is Emplifi's 10,110-Reel Facebook study (brand pages, correlational), the 2023 TikTok and MetrixLab hook study (ads), and sixty years of speech-perception experiments that were never run on vertical video. Everything about "the first 1.5 seconds of a face-to-camera reel" is therefore inference from those plus the account's own skip rate.

## 13. First 1.5 s: spoken line, visual change, text, or all three?

**Answer.** The measured evidence says: a human face plus a human voice starting inside the first three seconds is the single largest lift, and it is the voice more than the face. Text in the opening helps music-led video and slightly hurts speech-led video, so for a talking head the order of priority is voice first, face moving second, text third and small. The strongest hook is a spoken sentence that is already mid-thought while the face is already doing something (turning, reaching, reacting), with one short title lockup as a redundancy for muted viewers, not as the main event. Nobody has measured 1.5 s against 3 s for talking heads; the 1 to 1.5 s figure comes from Shorts practitioners describing the swipe decision.

**Sources.**
- Emplifi, Facebook Reels data (10,110 Reels, 704 brand pages). Speech beginning in the first 3 s lifted 3 s retention 11.7%, 10 s retention 24.7%, 30 s retention 6.6% over music-only; a person visible for at least 1 s in the first 3 s added about 3.6% at 3 s and about 10% at 10 s, fading by 30 s. https://emplifi.io/resources/facebook-reels-data/ and coverage at https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/human-speech-and-presence-help-drive-reels-engagement/ . **Measured** (correlational, brand pages, Facebook not Instagram).
- TikTok and MetrixLab "How to Hook" study (2023), reported by Vidmob and Adweek: direct-to-camera talent footage gave a 14% lift in 2 s view-through and "50% increased hooking power". https://vidmob.com/resource/tiktok-hook-analysis and https://www.adweek.com/adweek-wire/report-reveals-key-visual-and-creative-ad-strategies-for-hooking-tiktok-audiences/ . **Measured** (ads, secondhand figures).
- Mosseri, March 2025 (Inman write-up): the first three seconds are "super important", he calls that window the hook, and suggests text on the video as a first step. https://www.inman.com/2025/03/07/head-of-instagram-reveals-secrets-to-social-media-success/ . **Practitioner** (platform head, no numbers).
- Jenny Hoyos (1.6B views on 124 Shorts): hooks no more than 3 s, "retention doesn't matter as much as people think", the story and the twist carry it. https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook and https://podcast.creatorscience.com/jenny-hoyos/ . **Practitioner**.
- "On Shorts the swipe decision happens in about one second, so the hook is your first frame plus your first line." https://cursa.app/en/page/hook-engineering-in-the-first-second . **Folklore** (repeated, unsourced).
- dev.to "Hook Engineering" (0.84 correlation between 3 s retention and final views on 120 videos; "68% of TikTok views start muted"). https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9 . **Folklore** dressed as data; no method.

**Confidence:** medium on "voice and face beat text for a talking head" (two independent measured sources agree); low on any specific sub-second timing.

## 14. "Don't start with hi": why a greeting costs views, and does a mid-sentence open read as a trick to a 35-year-old man in Wyoming?

**Answer.** No study isolates "hey guys". The mechanism is inferable from the Emplifi and TikTok numbers: the lift comes from information arriving in the first seconds, and a greeting is seconds with no information, on a platform where the viewer did not click and so owes you nothing. YouTube's own Intro metric (viewers still present at 30 s) is the only platform-defined measure, and it is for long-form. On the second half: there is no age-split data on cold opens. The adjacent research on older adults and clickbait says people over 60 are about as sceptical of false headlines as the young, so the "older men hate tricks" idea is not supported as an age effect. What the hook literature does say, consistently, is that a cold open fails when it promises something the video does not deliver; a cold open that is simply the true middle of a true story is not read as a trick by anyone. The risk for Jake is not the cold open, it is a cold open on a line the reel cannot pay off.

**Sources.**
- YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention": Intro = share still watching after the first 30 s. https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415 . **Measured** (metric definition only).
- Creator Playbook, "Your intro sucks": skip the "hey guys", say what the viewer gets. https://www.creator-playbook.com/articles/your-intro-sucks-fix-first-30-seconds . **Practitioner**.
- Artiphik retention playbook: "cut all throat-clearing (greetings, logos, subscribe asks)". https://artiphik.com/blog/the-first-10-seconds-retention-playbook . **Practitioner**.
- Captions.ai: avoid opening with an intro, your name, or context the viewer didn't ask for. https://captions.ai/blog/how-to-make-instagram-reels . **Practitioner** (vendor).
- Outlier Kit: "you'll train viewers not to trust your hooks if you don't deliver something non-obvious." https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-hooks-and-retention/ . **Practitioner**.

## 15. The "re-hook": is the one-third / two-thirds re-hook measured anywhere, or inherited from YouTube?

**Answer.** Inherited, and not even precisely. No source traces "re-hook" to an origin or a dataset. In long-form YouTube practice it means a curiosity device every 90 s to 3 min, which has no meaning in a 60 s reel. The "two-thirds" placement shows up as advice to put the strongest moment two-thirds through so it rewards the people who stayed while most are still there, which is a reasonable guess, not a measurement. The one real datum near it is the Shorts average-percentage-viewed benchmark of about 74%, which people have back-formed into "most viewers leave in the last third, so re-hook before it". For a 30 to 90 s talking head the honest version is: one open loop in the first sentence, one turn in the middle, the answer at the end, and the retention chart in Insights will show whether the middle sagged.

**Sources.**

- Outlier Kit: strongest moment "roughly two-thirds of the way through". https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-hooks-and-retention/ . **Practitioner**.
- LensPOV and Air.io: rehook every 90 to 180 s or 2 to 3 min (long-form). https://lenspov.com/articles/youtube-retention-guide and https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/advanced-retention-editing-cutting-patterns-that-keep-viewers-past-minute-8 . **Practitioner**.

## 16. The title sentence burned top for 3 s: does on-screen text in the first second raise or lower hold for a face-to-camera video?

**Answer.** The one dataset that splits by audio type says text overlays correlate with slightly lower retention on speech-driven video (about minus 6% at 10 s and minus 4.5% at 30 s) and higher retention on music-driven video (plus 9.8% at 30 s). Pooled across all video the effect is small positive (plus 5% at 3 s, flat at 30 s). That is correlational and from brand pages, so it may partly mean "text-heavy brand reels are worse reels". The practitioner consensus runs the other way (always text in the first second because of muted viewing), but the "85% watch muted" figure behind it has no primary source. Jake's existing rule (one poster lockup on the hook, nothing else burned in on the fast tier) is consistent with the measured side: one short line, gone before it competes with the face. Nobody has published an A/B on title text for a talking head; Trial Reels or alternating days are how he would run one.

## 17. Words per minute, pause length, pitch range: what do top talking heads measure at, and what does speech research say about warmth and competence?

**Answer.** Nobody has published a measured corpus of short-form talking heads. The nearest measured figures: educational YouTubers run 178 to 185 wpm (Deepgram transcripts), popular TED talks average 173 (range 154 to 201), news audio rated "normal" at 170 to 190, and ordinary presenters have a median of about 111. The experimental literature is older but consistent: competence ratings rise roughly linearly with rate, while warmth (benevolence) peaks at the speaker's normal rate and drops when the voice is sped up (Smith, Brown, Strong and Rencher 1975). Faster speech raised credibility in Miller et al. 1976, but later work shows it can block processing of the actual argument. Pitch: higher mean pitch, wider range and more rate variation read as charismatic (Rosenberg and Hirschberg 2005; Biadsy et al. cross-culturally), with one caveat that male lecturers were rated more charismatic with a low range and slow rate. Falling sentence-final intonation signals confidence and helps persuasion only sometimes (Vaughan-Johnston et al. 2024). Pauses: a 1.2 s gap before a reply reads as less knowledgeable and less confident than 0.2 s (Matzinger 2025), and pauses inside a clause hurt perceived fluency where pauses between clauses do not. The practical target that falls out: about 160 to 180 wpm for explanation, dropping to the natural rate for the warm line, pauses at clause boundaries only, under a second, and let the pitch move.

## 18. Filler words, stumbles, restarts: authenticity signal or retention cost? Where is the line?

**Answer.** The line is measured, and it is more generous than editors assume. Laske and DiGennaro Reed 2024 seeded speeches with 0, 2, 5 and 12 disfluencies per minute: ratings held at 5 per minute and fell at 12, and the damage came from filler sounds (um, uh) rather than filler words (like, so). Niebuhr and Fischer's 68 investor pitches say cutting filled pauses only pays once a speaker is above about 8 per minute. Listener perception is otherwise negative in high-stakes settings: more fillers mean lower credibility, lower perceived readiness and certainty, and listeners assume anxiety that is not actually there. On the other side, "uh" measurably speeds recognition of the next word and hesitations orient attention (Fox Tree 2001; Collard et al. 2008), so a single honest stumble before the important noun is not a cost. No one has measured stumbles against Reels retention; the "lo-fi beats polished" claim is practitioner opinion. Rule: keep the one stumble that lands on a real word, cut the rest, and never cut so tight that the breaths disappear.

**Sources.**
- Laske and DiGennaro Reed 2024, Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis. https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-38819033 ; practitioner summary https://www.scienceofpeople.com/filler-words/ . **Measured**.
- Niebuhr and Fischer poster, 68 pitches, threshold above 8 per minute. https://www.sdu.dk/-/media/files/om_sdu/institutter/idk/prosody-pragmatics_files/niebuhr_fischer_poster_v1.pdf . **Measured** (poster).
- Lee and Papafragou (Penn): filled pauses lower perceived readiness and certainty regardless of expertise. https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8579j2fh . **Measured**.
- Christenfeld 1995: ums are not anxiety, but listeners assume they are. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02175503 . **Measured**.
- Kirkland et al., Interspeech 2023: disfluent public speech rated less competent, less confident and less sincere. https://www.speech.kth.se/tts-demos/kirkland23_interspeech.pdf . **Measured**.

## 19. Teleprompter tells and how to beat them

**Answer.** The tell is not the prompter, it is the eyes: the eye-contact literature puts the threshold where viewers notice you are not looking at them at 1.2 to 2.7 degrees horizontally and about 5 degrees vertically, with the best perceived contact when gaze lands about 2 degrees below lens centre. A phone prompter app with text sitting under a front camera, held at arm's length, is inside that tolerance only if the text block is narrow and the font large enough to stop lateral scanning ("shifty eyes" is the named failure). Beyond eye position, the tells are flat intonation, no breath pauses, and a neck that does not move. The practitioner fixes converge: bullets or phrases as cues rather than full sentences, one idea per line, big font, memorise the first and last lines so the open and close are to a human, read one or two lines ahead, record the hook last once warmed up, and write the way you talk. I could not verify the Jessica Carter prompter admission with a search today; treat it as Jacob's own observation. Note Jake's cab setup gives him an advantage: short notes on the sun visor, directly above the lens at 2 to 4 degrees, are nearly invisible.

**Sources.**

## 20. Talking to one person: evidence that second-person singular raises completion or shares

**Answer.** There is measured evidence that "you" raises liking and uptake of cultural products, and no measured evidence yet on Reels completion. Berger and Packard 2020 found songs with more second-person pronouns charted higher (4,200 Billboard songs) and, in experiments with 2,921 participants, were liked and bought more, with the mechanism being that "you" makes the listener think of a specific person in their own life. The parasocial literature adds that direct bodily address and direct gaze produce a stronger experience of parasocial interaction (Hartmann and Goldhoorn 2011; Dibble, Hartmann and Rosaen follow-ups), which is the plausible path to a share ("this is you" sent to one friend). Advertising work I know but could not re-verify today (Cruz, Leonhardt and Pezzuti 2017) found second-person pronouns raise involvement and brand attitude for customers who already have a relationship with the brand, which fits a follower audience better than a cold one. Jun Yuh's "write to one person" advice is practitioner method; no search returned him. Practical reading: "you", one named person, one situation; the Billboard effect suggests the gain is in liking and sends rather than in raw hold.

**Sources.**

## 21. Lowering status: self-deprecation, vulnerability and trust

**Answer.** The research supports exactly the kind of line Jake already uses, with two conditions. The pratfall effect (Aronson, Willerman and Floyd 1966; 48 male students) is that a blunder makes a competent person more likeable and a mediocre person less so; the blunder had no main effect on its own, and the participants believed it was accidental. So lowering status works after competence is on the table, and it works when it is true. Leader studies replicate the trust piece: self-deprecating humour raised trustworthiness and perceived leadership without being rated funnier (Hoption, Barling and Turner 2013, n = 155), with trust mediating effectiveness (Gkorezis and Bellou 2016) and the same in a 369-dyad field study (2022); every one of them warns that too much reads as insincere. The influencer literature says negative self-disclosure and admitted failures can raise perceived authenticity, but sceptical followers discount it and sponsored self-disclosure can look calculated. The "beautiful mess effect" (Bruk, Scholl and Bless 2018, JPSP: we judge our own vulnerability more harshly than observers judge it) is the paper behind the Brené Brown framing; I know it but could not re-verify it today. Net: "I don't own a shop, I work at one" is the right shape, because it concedes status while asserting the credential in the same breath.

## What it means for a one-man diesel creator filming a daily talking head in his truck cab

Rules, each one testable against Skip Rate and the retention chart in Insights.

1. **Voice before frame one.** The first audible thing is Jake mid-sentence, not room tone, not a breath, not music. Emplifi's biggest lift was speech starting inside 3 s; start it inside 0.5 s. Test: compare 3 s hold on reels with speech at 0.0 to 0.5 s versus 1 s or later.
2. **The first sentence contains the noun and the person.** "You" or a named person and the concrete thing (the 10 mm, the invoice, the boss) in sentence one. No greeting, no name, no "so today". Test: skip rate on reels whose first five words contain a noun versus those that do not.
3. **Cold open only on a line the reel pays off.** Open in the middle of the true story, never on a promise. The trick is not the cold open, it is the unpaid promise. Test: shares per reach on cold-open reels that close the loop versus ones that end on a question.
4. **One lockup, three words to seven, gone by 3 s.** Speech-led video loses a little hold to persistent text. Keep the poster line as a muted-viewer redundancy and nothing else burned in. Test: alternate weeks with and without the lockup, same slot, same length.
5. **Forget the one-third / two-thirds re-hook.** For 30 to 90 s there is one loop at the top, one turn in the middle, the answer in the last ten seconds. Read the retention chart; if it sags at a point, that point gets a turn next time, not a formula.
6. **Pace 160 to 180 wpm for the explanation, drop to his own speed for the human line.** Competence goes with pace, warmth goes with his natural rate. Count words on a transcript once a week.
7. **Pauses under one second, at the ends of clauses only.** A 1.2 s gap before the key line reads as unsure; a half-second after it reads as confident. Never cut a pause that sits after the payoff.
8. **Let the pitch move and end the key sentences falling.** Wide range reads as charisma; falling finals read as certainty. Flat delivery is the prompter tell even without a prompter.
9. **Up to five ums a minute is free; above eight, cut.** Keep the one stumble that lands on a real word. Never cut the breaths out.
10. **Lower status only after the credential is in the room.** "I don't own a shop, I work at one" works because the shop is in frame. Once per reel at most; the studies all say too much reads as fake.

### Dash card (tape it to the dash)

- Lens at eye level. Eyes on the lens or 2 degrees under it. Notes on the visor, directly above.
- Notes are five bullets max, one idea per line, big. First line and last line memorised. Say them to a person.
- Say the first word before you hit record in your head. No "hey", no "so", no name.
- First sentence: who (you, or one name) and what (the thing). Then the turn. Then the answer.
- Talk to one guy. The one who would send this to his buddy.
- Speed: a notch above normal for the facts. Normal for the line that is about him.
- Pause after the payoff, not before it. Under a second.
- Let the voice fall at the end of the sentence that matters.
- One stumble stays. Breaths stay. Ums over eight a minute go.
- One self-drop per reel, after the credential, never before.
- Record the hook last if it is not landing. Stop when the last line is said. No outro.
