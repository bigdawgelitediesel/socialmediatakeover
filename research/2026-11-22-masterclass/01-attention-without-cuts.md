# Cluster A: holding attention in a talking head without many cuts

Written 2026-11-22 for the masterclass run. Questions 1 to 6 of `00-questions.md`. Method: WebSearch only (WebFetch resolved nothing in this container; alexdaly.substack.com and opus.pro both failed). Every claim is graded **measured** (a study, platform data, a retention curve, an A/B test), **practitioner** (an editor, creator or course saying what they do) or **folklore** (a number repeated without a source). Two local receipts are used as practitioner evidence: the Mariah "Think Like An Editor" breakdown (`research/reel-references/2026-11-22-Derd9IvoWaj/BREAKDOWN.md`, 50K likes, 68 s, eight notebook inserts, no transitions) and the Caleb Ralston 90 s one-shot (`research/reel-references/2026-11-22-DdmJNQJp81s/BREAKDOWN.md`, 19.6K likes, no cuts, headline per rule). The search budget ran out before the last three queries (Reesa Teesa recording process, Guo 2014 MOOC data, "edit for emotion" interview editors); those gaps are marked.

Short version: the cut frequency rule is not where the evidence is. The measured work says attention is held by **change that the viewer's prediction system has to update on** (new information, a new event, a movement the eye follows, a change in the voice), and that a cut is only one way to make that change, and the one most often made invisible. Nobody has published a retention curve that shows a face held for 8 s losing viewers that a cut would have kept.

## 1. What holds attention in a long single take: frame change, voice change, or information change?

**Answer.** The measured literature points to information change first, voice change second, and frame change third, which is the reverse of what the creator guides assume. Event segmentation research (Zacks) shows the brain resets attention at points where its prediction about what happens next fails, and most cuts in a film are not such points; only about one in ten cuts is an event boundary, and viewers literally do not see most of the rest. Blink synchronisation research (Nakano) shows attention slackens at the end of an action and tightens at the next one, with or without a cut, and the effect vanishes when sound is removed, so the voice carries the segmentation. Eye tracking on speakers (Gullberg and Holmqvist) shows viewers spend over 90 percent of the time on the face and under 0.5 percent on the hands, yet gesture still measurably improves comprehension (Hostetter meta-analysis, moderate effect, larger when the gesture adds information the words do not), so gesture works peripherally and does not need to be "looked at" to work. The honest statement is: new information every few seconds is what the evidence supports; movement in the frame and in the voice is what makes that information register; a cut is an optional way to mark it.

- Zacks, Speer, Swallow and Maley, "The brain's cutting-room floor: segmentation of narrative cinema" (2010), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2819197 . Cortical response spikes at situational discontinuities, not at cuts as such; about 10 percent of cuts are scene boundaries. **Measured.**
- Magliano and Zacks (2011), via https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s41235-019-0188-x . Commercial editing is shaped to bridge low-level visual breaks; cuts that keep the situation cohesive go unnoticed. **Measured.**
- Nakano et al., "Synchronization of spontaneous eyeblinks while viewing video stories" (2009), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2817301 . Blinks synchronise at low-attention moments (end of an action, long shots), not at scene breaks; no synchronisation for a story read aloud or a background video. **Measured.**
- Nakano and Kitazawa (2010), https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00221-010-2387-z . Listeners blink 0.25 to 0.5 s after a speaker in close-up with sound; effect gone without sound. **Measured.**
- Gullberg and Holmqvist (2006), summarised in https://escholarship.org/content/qt3c59r5rd/qt3c59r5rd.pdf . Face over 90 percent of fixations, gesture under 0.5 percent; smaller screens let gesture be read without leaving the face. **Measured.**
- Hostetter, "When do gestures communicate? A meta-analysis" (2011, 63 samples) and Dargue, Sweller and Jones (2019, 83 samples), https://humintell.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/Dargue-et-al.-2019.pdf . Moderate benefit to listener comprehension; larger for non-redundant gestures. **Measured.**
- PROSOCOGNITION (EU project), https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/190888-prosody-strategies-to-improve-attention-and-memory-of-audio-messages . Pitch contrast, a moderate number of stressed words and about 180 words per minute raised psychophysiological attention measures for audio messages. **Measured, audio only.**
- Watson et al. on filled pauses, via https://www.tes.com/magazine/archive/why-prosody-powerful-tool-teachers . Listeners who heard "um" retold a story better than those who heard silence or coughs. **Measured, small.**
- Open loops: Ghibellini and Meier 2025 meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik effect, https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html . The memory advantage for interrupted tasks does not replicate reliably; the urge to resume (Ovsiankina) does. So "open loop" is a real pull to come back, not a proven hold on attention inside one video. **Measured, against the folklore.**
- "Pattern interrupt every 4 seconds holds 58 percent vs 41 percent for static talking heads", https://livecounts.io/blog/youtube/how-mrbeast-uses-pattern-interrupts-every-4-seconds and https://adamconnell.me/improve-video-retention-social-media/ . No sample, no method. **Folklore.**
- Mariah, "Think Like An Editor" (local breakdown). 68 s, one locked shot, change every 8 to 10 s from a notebook insert on the noun, no transitions, 50K likes. **Practitioner, with a result.**

Confidence: medium. The mechanism evidence is strong and consistent; none of it was measured on vertical short form.

## 2. Does Murch's rule of six survive vertical short form, and what is "cut for emotion" in a talking head?

**Answer.** Murch's weights (emotion 51, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, screen plane 5, space 4) are a working editor's estimate, not a measurement, and his blink idea has since been partly confirmed: Nakano found blinks cluster at attentional breakpoints, and Smith's edit blindness work found continuity-rule cuts are the ones viewers miss. The order probably holds in short form with one change argued by practitioners and supported by Smith's theory: eye-trace moves up, because on a small vertical screen with short shots the viewer's gaze position at the cut decides whether the cut is felt at all. "Cut for emotion" in a talking head means the cut (or the insert, or the punch) lands where the speaker's state changes, which Murch said is where the speaker and the viewer both blink: the end of a thought, the beat before the turn, the moment the face changes. It does not mean cutting on a schedule. Search budget ran out before a dedicated interview-editor source on "performance over coverage" was found; the Princeton and EditShare guides below are the nearest.

- Murch's six criteria, https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/walter-murch-rule-of-six/ and https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch . Percentages, trade-off order (give up the bottom first), blink as cut point from The Conversation (1974). **Practitioner, canonical.**
- Blink as cut point, https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/cutting-on-the-blink-editing-tips-from-walter-murch . A person blinks at a new whole thought or emotion. **Practitioner.**
- Nakano 2009 (above). Blinks cluster at attentional breakpoints across viewers. **Measured support for Murch.**
- Smith, "The Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity" (2012), https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21187/ , and Smith and Henderson 2008 on edit blindness, https://doi.org/10.16910%2Fjemr.2.2.6 . Cuts that follow continuity rules are missed more; much of the blindness is inattention, not saccade suppression; edit blindness largely disappears without audio. **Measured.**
- Eye-trace should weigh more in fast online video, https://nofilmschool.com/2018/08/editing-eye-trace-mind-rule-six-incorrect and https://artlist.io/blog/eye-trace-and-rule-of-six-editing/ . Argument from screen size and shot length. **Practitioner.**
- Applying the rule to non-narrative content, https://nofilmschool.com/editing-emotion-using-walter-murchs-rule-six-non-narrative-content . **Practitioner.**

Confidence: medium on the order, high that "cut for emotion" means cut on the state change and not on a clock.

## 3. Is "a visual change every 3 to 5 seconds" measured or repeated?

**Answer.** Repeated. No primary source was found in any search; the number moves with the teller (1 to 2 s, 1.5 to 2.5 s, 2 to 3 s, 3 to 4 s, 4 to 5 s for Reels, 8 to 15 s for YouTube) and is attributed to "internal agency studies" or "TikTok's creator resources" without a document. The likely ancestry is three real findings mashed together: Cutting's measurement that Hollywood average shot length fell from 8 to 10 s in the 1960s to 3 to 4 s by 2005 (a description of films, not a prescription for attention, and the 1/f pattern he found has no relation to how much audiences liked the films); MrBeast's editing style, described in blogs as "a shot rarely runs past four seconds"; and the Buffer "pattern interrupt" anecdote repeated by Backlinko with no design. The only controlled-looking number on inserts is a vendor's single test (3 to 7 percent retention lift at the moment of a relevant insert), which argues for inserts that illustrate, not for a schedule.

- Cutting, DeLong and Nothelfer 2010, https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797610361679/ and Cutting, DeLong, Brunick 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5849648 . Shot length history; 1/f structure; "no relationship to how much people liked the films". **Measured, descriptive, not a rule.**
- https://blitzcutai.com/blog/3-second-rule-tiktok-jump-cuts . Jump cuts every 3 to 4 s, "internal studies from top TikTok agencies", none named; table gives 4 to 5 s for Reels. **Folklore.**
- https://www.skool.com/lmgs-ai-academy/the-pattern-interrupt-why-your-videos-get-swiped?p=1f56ca82 . Visual shift every 1.5 to 2.5 s. **Folklore.**
- https://livecounts.io/blog/youtube/how-mrbeast-uses-pattern-interrupts-every-4-seconds . 2 to 4 s average hold, no data source. **Folklore.**
- https://backlinko.com/hub/youtube/retention . Buffer added angle changes, B-roll and graphics and retention "skyrocketed"; no numbers, no control. **Practitioner anecdote.**
- https://www.opus.pro/blog/does-b-roll-increase-watch-time . One creator test, 3 to 7 percent lift at insert moments, only when the insert illustrated the point. Vendor. **Weak measured.**
- https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9 . "84 percent of final view count decided by second three", no study. **Folklore.**

Confidence: high that it is unsourced; medium on the ancestry.

## 4. What do the one-take masters of 2024 to 2026 actually do?

**Answer.** They share a fixed set (a car seat, a desk, a kitchen, a camp chair), a camera that does not move, a distance of chest-up or waist-up, and a change supply that comes from the content: a numbered structure, a rating, a reveal per episode, a catchphrase that marks the start, and the face and hands doing the work. Reesa Teesa's 50 parts were just under 10 minutes each, posted within days, built as a playlist so they autoplayed, and she said she made them to be listened to "as an audiobook, chapter by chapter"; viewers reported playing all 50 while washing dishes, which means the hold was narrative and vocal, not visual. Keith Lee sits in a car with a takeout bag, opens on the same line every time, rates each item out of 10, and delivers in a flat monotone; the suspense is the number. Nara Smith's change is the recipe step and a near-whisper that began as not waking her sleeping children and was kept when comments liked it. Drew Afualo opens every video on the same "Heeeeey" and laugh, then reacts to a clip, in a car or at home; the structure is response, not montage. Caleb Ralston (local) held one locked wide for 88 s with a new headline per rule and a countdown ring. Mariah (local) held one desk shot with eight notebook inserts. No source confirmed "one take, no edits" for Keith Lee or Reesa; TechCrunch notes car confessionals can be rehearsed and cut; treat "one take" as the look, not a verified production fact. Hank Green and Corporate Natalie turned up no format analysis in search; the Alex Daly "Dissecting HankTok" post exists but could not be fetched.

## 5. When is a jump cut invisible and when does it read as robotic?

**Answer.** Smith's edit blindness work gives the rule: a cut is missed when attention is elsewhere at the moment of the cut and when sound continues across it; it is seen when the image jumps while the eye is parked on the thing that jumps, which in a talking head is the face. So the invisible jump cut keeps the eye on the same spot (nearest eye at the same screen position), changes scale enough that the brain reads it as a new shot rather than a glitch (EditShare: more than about 30 percent; the 15 to 20 percent figure in talking-head guides is for the softer "alternate framing at sentence breaks" move, and no source measured either), and lands on an audio event (the start of a word, a breath, a head turn) rather than mid-vowel. The robotic read comes from three things: cuts every few words so the breathing is gone, cuts that happen mid-gesture so the hands teleport, and the uncanny-valley effect the Nature video-glitch study found, where short freezes and jumps during face-to-face speech lowered perceived trustworthiness, and did not when the face was not the subject. Documentary interview editors hide cuts with a punch-in on a matched eye-line, a cutaway (B-roll or the listener), an L-cut so the audio leads the picture, a morph cut of 4 to 8 frames for small jumps, and by choosing the cut point at a natural pause or head move.

- Smith and Henderson 2008 (edit blindness) and Smith 2012 (above). **Measured.**
- Smith and Martin-Portugues Santacreu 2016, https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21215/ . Match-action cuts are hidden by motion and audio; without audio the blindness mostly disappears. **Measured.**
- Video glitches lower trust during face-to-face speech only (Nature, reported Dec 2025), https://www.npr.org/2025/12/03/nx-s1-5628360/glitchy-video-calls-zoom-teams-creepy-mistrust . Glitches inserted at pauses; uncanny feeling; no effect during screen share. **Measured, adjacent (glitches, not edits).**
- Visual distortions lower credibility via processing fluency, three preregistered experiments (2026 preprint), https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13846v2 . **Measured, adjacent.**
- EditShare, https://editshare.com/post/5-fundamental-editing-techniques/ . Line up the nearest eye; punch more than 30 percent so it stops reading as a jump cut; cut on a word to combine eye-trace with an audio match; head turns and eye-line shifts as cut points; shoot above delivery resolution. **Practitioner.**
- Princeton remote ethnography guide, https://commons.princeton.edu/remote-ethnography/editing-remote-interviews/ . Jump cuts to remove ums and silences; morph cut 4 to 8 frames, too short still looks choppy. **Practitioner.**
- Talking-head guides, https://1of10.com/blog/talking-head-video/ and https://jupitrr.com/how-to/edit-talking-head-videos . Alternate full frame and a 15 to 20 percent punch at sentence breaks; a silence-removal cut lands at the end of the last word and is usually still slow. **Practitioner.**
- L-cut and J-cut, https://vimeo.com/blog/post/guide-to-film-cuts and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L_cut . **Practitioner, standard.**
- Viewer feedback that punch zooms distract, https://creativecow.net/?p=777101 . **Anecdote.**
- Nofilmschool, six ways to hide jump cuts, https://nofilmschool.com/easy-ways-to-hide-jump-cuts-your-next-video . **Practitioner.**

Confidence: high on the mechanism (eye position plus audio continuity), medium on the exact punch percentage.

## 6. How much B-roll: cover every noun or one insert per idea?

**Answer.** One insert per idea, on the noun, is what the evidence supports; covering every noun is a style with no retention data behind it. The only test with numbers (vendor, one creator) found a 3 to 7 percent lift at the insert moment and only when the insert illustrated the point being made, which is Mariah's notebook rule exactly (eight inserts in 68 s, each on its noun, nothing else). Hostetter's meta-analysis adds that non-redundant visuals help comprehension more than redundant ones, so an insert that shows what the words already say is the weakest kind. Guidance from the talking-head trade says two to four seconds per insert and warns that covering the face for long stretches removes the thing the viewer came for. YouTube's own retention tool lets a creator check this per video: compare the curve slope before and after each insert; a flat run after an insert is a working insert, a dip right after it is an insert that broke the thread. No published curve was found that separates "face holds" from "insert lands" across many channels; this has to be measured on the account. The search budget ran out before Guo et al. 2014 (MOOC engagement, 6.9 million sessions) could be pulled; from memory of that paper, talking-head segments interleaved with slides out-engaged slides alone and median engagement fell after about six minutes, which supports "face plus occasional insert" over either alone, but treat it as unverified in this file.

- https://www.opus.pro/blog/does-b-roll-increase-watch-time . 3 to 7 percent lift at insert moments when relevant; suggests 35 to 50 percent coverage, a figure from one test by an editing-tool vendor. **Weak measured, with a sales interest.**
- Hostetter 2011 and Dargue 2019 (above). Non-redundant visual information helps more. **Measured, transfers by analogy.**
- YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention", https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415 . Dips, spikes and flat stretches; how to read an insert's effect. **Platform tool.**
- https://www.merra.ai/blog/talking-head-videos . Trust comes from seeing you; cutaways two to four seconds at a time. **Practitioner.**
- https://uppbeat.io/blog/youtube-growth/youtube-analytics/youtube-audience-retention and https://vidiq.com/es/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/ . B-roll as pattern interrupt; about 30 percent average view as a realistic long-form target. **Practitioner.**
- Mariah local breakdown. Eight inserts in 68 s, one prop, on the noun, 50K likes; Caleb local breakdown, zero cuts, a card per noun in a fixed slot, 19.6K likes. **Practitioner, with results.**

Confidence: medium. The direction (on the noun, sparse, non-redundant) is well supported; the quantity is not measured anywhere public.

## What it means for a one-man diesel creator filming a daily talking head in his truck cab

Each rule is written so it can be checked against `skip_rate_pct` and average watch time in `posts.tsv` after ten episodes.

1. **Plan the information beats, not the cut points.** Script the take as a list of six to ten new facts or turns, one every 5 to 8 s. If a stretch of 10 s adds nothing new, cut the words, not the picture. Test: episodes with a beat every 8 s or less should sit under the 40 percent skip gate more often than those without.
2. **Let the voice do the segmenting.** Pitch down and pause for half a second before each turn; stress one word per beat; aim near 180 words a minute when explaining and slower when landing a line. Keep the ums. Test: compare watch time on episodes read flat against episodes with marked turns.
3. **Hands in frame, chest-up, camera still.** Gesture is read peripherally and helps comprehension even though nobody looks at it; the frame has to include the hands for that to work. Test: watch time on chest-up framings against face-only framings.
4. **One insert per idea, on the noun, 2 to 4 s, never covering a sentence that carries emotion.** The legal pad on the dash is the whole budget. Never add an insert that only repeats the words. Test: skip rate and watch time on episodes with 0, 3 and 6 inserts.
5. **When a cut is unavoidable, hide it the documentary way.** Same eye position, punch of 30 percent or more (shoot 4K for a 1080 delivery), cut on the first consonant of a word or on a head turn, keep the room tone running underneath. Never cut mid-gesture or mid-breath. Test: comments mentioning "choppy" or "AI edit" should be zero.
6. **Cut for the state change, not the clock.** If the take needs trimming, cut where his face changes (the beat before the opinion, the moment after the punchline), which is where Murch and Nakano both put the viewer's blink. One or two such cuts in a 60 s take; zero is fine.
7. **Same seat, same distance, same opening line.** Keith Lee, Drew Afualo and Reesa hold attention with a fixed set and a fixed ritual so the change is all content. Pick one opening line for the cab series and keep it for thirty days. Test: completion rate on episode 20 against episode 2.
8. **Open loops bring people back; they do not hold them.** The replication data says the pull to resume is real and the in-video memory effect is not. Use the loop at the end of the episode to name the next one; do not rely on a teased payoff to carry a dull middle.
9. **Audio continuity is the real continuity.** Edit blindness disappears without sound, and glitches during face-to-face speech cost trust. Record the DJI Mic clean, never let a cut make a click or a jump in the room tone, and keep music out from under the voice in the cab.
10. **Measure before adopting any interval rule.** Nothing in the public record justifies "a change every 3 to 5 s" for this format. After ten cab episodes, plot skip rate and average watch time against inserts per minute and cuts per minute from the edit logs; the account's own curve is the only evidence that exists for this question.
