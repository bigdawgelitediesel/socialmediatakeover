# The El Coyote editing doctrine: a human reel that holds without the slot machine

Written 2026-11-22 at the end of a two-hour run Jacob asked for: "go through every study, everything you know about social media, create inspired questions, research what you need to get those answered by multiple sources, become a master of the research and use that to create the ultimate editor of reels and social media master. Dive into what you have done and learn from it."

Rules and lint scores do not judge the picture; the look check in its section 9 runs before Jacob sees anything.

Where this file and an older rule disagree, this file wins; the eighteen conflicts it resolves are listed in section 15. ## 1. The one idea

**Change, not cut.** The viewer stays for a change they care about. A cut is one way to make a change and the most expensive one, because every unmotivated cut spends a little trust (the glitch-and-trust study, Mariah's 50K-like receipt, the account's own 14.8 versus 1.4 percent engagement on talking heads against cut-heavy sponsor reels). Across 131 studied reels, cut rate does not separate winners from losers: the top and bottom thirds sit at 16.6 and 14.5 cuts a minute, and inside a third the range is 0 to 49. What separates them is whether something the viewer cares about is changing every few seconds, and the winners make that change with a hand, a prop, a written word, a camera move, a sound, or a turn in the voice long before they reach for a cut.

So the editor's job, in order: write a script where the information changes every five to eight seconds; film it so the frame changes with the information (hands, prop, page, light, a move); let the voice segment it; and cut only to remove, on a breath or a gesture, hiding the join with sound. A hold is alive for as long as something in it moves or sounds, and dead the moment nothing does, whatever its length.

## 2. Before the camera: the story

1. **Write the last line first.** The last line is the five-second moment, the one change in the teller, said plainly (Dicks). Then write the first line as its opposite, the thing he believed before. The reel is the distance between them.
2. **But and therefore, never and then.** Read the script aloud; strike every sentence that joins with "and then." Three beats only: normal, explosion, new normal (Hall; Pixar rule 4). If a beat can be cut without the story breaking, cut it.
3. **Zoom into the moment (Humm).** Location in one phrase, the verb, the raw thought, the emotion shown on the body, the exact words someone said. At least three of the five in the first 20 s. Never summarize what you can replay.
4. **One loop, opened in sentence one, closed in the last ten seconds.** At most one small loop, closed within 25 s. The replication data says loops bring people back between episodes; they do not carry a dull middle inside one. Never end unresolved.
5. **Stakes a stranger feels, in the first ten seconds:** money, hours, a Saturday, cold mornings, the boss, the wife, pride. Hypothetical loss transports nobody; a number does.
6. **One person, you and I, us against the problem.** Name one viewer in sentence one. "You" for what they want, "I" for the failures, then us against the thing (the part, the catalogue, the 10 mm). Never you against them.
7. **Say the thing they already think.** The most shared line names a thought the viewer has had and never said. Recognition, not news. If nobody in the audience has felt it, it is a save, not a send.
8. **Broad emotion, niche detail.** Broad topics earn reach (Kier: relationships, family, money, work); narrow detail earns trust (Kallaway: same avatar, same topics, or the sample goes to the wrong 200 and the next post pays). The resolution is a universal feeling carried by a specific object: the invoice, the socket, the truck that runs and cannot go anywhere.
9. **Credential inside a working sentence, never a pilot line.** "I order these for a living, and I still bought the wrong one." The credential comes before the mistake, because the pratfall only helps the already competent.
10. **Say the downside out loud.** Every "what I put on my truck" names one thing it does not do. Two-sided messages measure higher on credibility, and the whole auto audience's prior is that the trade hides things.
11. **Demand before diligence (Trinder).** Before a series is built, find three reels in the lane that already prove people watch the subject, and two that failed with the same format, and know why. Position the unfamiliar mechanism around the outcome they already want: nobody searches "lift pump," everybody wants the truck to start on a cold morning.
12. **Homework for life, cab edition.** One real moment a day written in the phone before the drive home. Tomorrow's talking head is today's best moment, not today's topic.

## 3. The first three seconds

Kier Porter's 112-reel sample and the independent data agree on the shape; file 10 has the per-item verdicts.

1. **Voice before frame one.** Mid-sentence inside 0.5 s. The biggest measured lift in the only large talking-head dataset is speech starting inside 3 s; the house starts it before the picture settles. No greeting, no name, no "so today."
2. **Something moves or enters by 0.33 s.** The lockup landing, a hand, the part coming up, a head turn, a door. On a seated talking head the sentence is the opener and the lockup is the move; a static face with a noun by 0.9 s beats a walk-in with "so."
3. **The first sentence contains the person and the noun.** "You" or one named person, and the concrete thing. First noun by 0.9 s.
4. **The title sentence, three to seven words, with a number, upper third, gone by 3 s.** Written as a flat claim or an identity or pain line ("the kind of guy who buys the cheap filter twice"), never a question. Readable at a glance, high contrast, never under the right rail or the bottom overlay.
5. **Open only on a line the reel pays off.** The cold open is not the trick; the unpaid promise is. A 35-year-old in Wyoming forgives a mid-sentence start and does not forgive a bait.
6. **No scheduled re-hook.** The one-third, two-thirds device is inherited from YouTube long-form and measured nowhere. One loop at the top, one turn in the middle, the answer last. Read the retention chart; where it sags, that point gets a turn next time.
7. **Write the lockup as a name-call, not a topic (file 10).** "For the guy still running the stock pump" beats "CP4 failure explained": identity first, the pain in the same breath. Hooks built on "you" ran 9.6x an account's typical views against 6.2x for "I" (Content Labs, 14,424 videos); a number in the hook adds almost nothing unless the number is the story.
8. **Rueful beats angry.** Sad words out-clicked angry ones across 105,000 Upworthy tests, and the relatability research says a mild shared grievance bonds and a rant does not. The tone of the open is "I did this too," never "you people."
9. **Rotate the set once a week.** After ten episodes the cab is a known frame; the F600 cab in the bay, the parts room, the counter after close, a Class 8 cab are pattern interrupts that are still his. "I work there" when the shop is in frame.
10. **What the six-things sample cannot say.** Kier counted what 112 winners shared with no losers in the sample, so it is prevalence, not lift; the measured side (Emplifi's 10,110 Reels, the TikTok and MetrixLab hook study, Content Labs) agrees on speech at once, a face, "you," and legible text, is silent on the timed angle change, and says layered text slightly lowers retention on speech-led video. One lockup, off by 3 s, then captions.

## 4. Delivery as a human

1. **160 to 180 words a minute for the facts, his own speed for the human line.** Competence rises with pace; warmth peaks at natural rate. Count a transcript once a week.
2. **Pauses under a second, at the ends of clauses, and one held beat (0.6 to 1.0 s) after the line that turns the story.** A long pause before the key line reads as unsure; a short one after it reads as certain. Nobody in diesel holds a pause; it is the cheapest device there is.
3. **Let the pitch move and end the sentences that matter falling.** Flat delivery is the prompter tell even without a prompter.
4. **Up to five ums a minute is free; above eight, cut.** Keep one stumble that lands on a real word. Never cut the breaths.
5. **Eyes on the lens or two degrees under it.** Five bullets on the visor directly above the lens, first and last lines memorized, hook recorded last if it is not landing. A full script makes a strong expert worse on camera; script tight only where the copy must be tight.
6. **Hands in frame, chest up.** Gesture is read peripherally and helps comprehension even though nobody looks at it; the frame has to include the hands.
7. **Low status once per episode, after the credential.** "I don't own a shop. I work at one." Too much reads as fake.
8. **Shared grievance, held lightly, about a thing, not a person.** Mild shared annoyance bonds strangers faster than praise; strong negativity does not.
9. **Comedy: write the joke backwards from one turn, land it on beat three, keep the face straight, stop.** If the bit needs a second person, the viewer is the second person.
10. **Same seat, same distance, same opening ritual for thirty days.** Keith Lee, Afualo, Reesa, Fisher, Carter, Mariah: the set does the branding so the edit does not have to, and the change is all content.

## 5. The hold: keeping them without cutting

1. **Plan the information beats, not the cut points.** Six to ten new facts or turns in a 60 s take, one every five to eight seconds. If ten seconds add nothing new, cut the words, not the picture.
2. **One physical device per episode, and cut only to it.** The legal pad on the dash (Mariah: eight pages in 68 s), the part in the hand, a numbered card, a countdown ring, the phone with the comment. Never two devices. The notebook is the default: your own hand writing the point in Sharpie, top-down, after the take; five to eight inserts on a trust episode, none on a funny one, each 2 to 4 s, in on the noun, out when the point lands, never over a sentence that carries the emotion.
3. **Numbered progress the viewer can see.** Fisher's three steps, Caleb's five rules and ring, Sam Gaudet's 1/5 to 5/5. A visible finish line is the honest version of the re-hook.
4. **Sound carries the hold.** Reesa is an audiobook, rust_ridge's 7.3 rattle is the soundtrack. If the episode does not work with the phone face down, it is not ready. Engine off, windows up, the cab is the booth; record the cab tone and the idle once.
5. **The camera move replaces the cut.** In every truck winner one person could shoot, the change was a walk, a half orbit, a push in on the part, never an edit. ActiveTrack from the passenger headrest is a move.
6. **The voice does the segmenting.** Pitch down and pause before each turn; stress one word per beat. The blink research says viewers reset attention at the end of an action in the sound, not at the cut.
7. **Open loops bring people back; they do not hold them.** Use the loop at the end to name the next episode. Do not rely on a teased payoff to carry a slow middle.
8. **A hold is dead the moment nothing moves or sounds.** Harley Trade died in 3.5 s of quiet truck; Westen holds a crank for 13 s. Length is not the variable.

## 6. When you do cut

1. **Cut when the information changes, never on a clock.** The 2 to 4 s cadence belongs to one lane, the sold AI-edit talking head, and that lane is the bottom third of the library on likes.
2. **Cut to remove, on a breath, a head turn, a hand up, or the first consonant of a word.** Never mid-gesture, never mid-breath, never mid-payoff. One or two such cuts in a 60 s take; zero is fine.
3. **Hide the join with sound, not with a zoom.** Edit blindness disappears without audio continuity: room tone runs under every cut (30 ms lead, 60 ms tail), the DJI Mic stays clean, no click. A punch-in hides a cut only on a locked camera and only at 30 percent or more (shoot 4K for 1080); never punch on handheld, never both a punch and a cut on the same join.
4. **A story told over footage cuts to whatever the sentence just named and holds until the next sentence names something else.** That can be every one to three seconds (Oversteer, DriveHub) and it is fine because the voice carries it. A montage cuts once per bar on the beat, and the montage is the pride slot, not the growth slot.
5. **If a cut cannot be named for what it shows, it is a transition, and the house does not use those.** No whip, no zoom-blur, no flash, no SFX on cuts or text. The only sounds beyond the voice are things in the cab or the shop, at most one per reel.
6. **B-roll amplifies the feeling, one insert per idea.** It does not have to illustrate the exact noun; it must never repeat the words. The only numeric test that exists found a small lift at inserts that added information and none at decoration.

## 7. Sound

- Voice at -14 LUFS integrated, true peak -1 dBTP, loudness range under 8 LU. A -16 upload is safe; never push past -12, the player takes the gain back and keeps the squash. Measure with the loudnorm summary on the delivered file.
- One second of room tone before the first word and after the last, every take, and under every cut.
- Voice only by default on the daily talking head. When a song is wanted (memes, montages, lyric formats) it is added inside Instagram and sits about 15 dB under the voice, roughly 15 to 25 percent on the app slider against original audio at 100; check on the phone speaker.
- Silence is placed, not left: one held beat after the turn, nothing else over 0.4 s.

## 8. Text on screen

- Talking heads are captioned: one third of plays are muted, every talking-head winner studied captions, the one first-party test measured +12 percent view time. Phrase captions, 2 to 4 words, 24 characters, one line, sentence case as spoken, Poppins SemiBold 68 px (64 to 80), white, two-layer shadow, no stroke, no pill, no karaoke, no per-word colour flash. Block centre y 1190 (window 1050 to 1360 chosen from the chin line), bottom never below 1400, nothing inside the right 130 px, never on the face, caption-free under the lockup for the first 3 s. At most three tan accent words per reel.
- **Beat words (Jacob's call, 2026-11-23, from @claudiorivera.910, rule 118):** on top of the plain captions, one big word per beat, Archivo Black in cream, coral or tan, Shrikhand for a flavour word, pop in and hold, placed by the face in each frame (above the head, else the clear side, else the chest band), never on the face. Every 4 s on a talking head, every 2 to 2.5 s on a skit or loop. This is the emphasis layer, not karaoke: the plain captions still carry every word.
- Action and sound reels: one lockup or nothing (ten of fourteen diesel winners burn no captions). Memes: the confession line only.
- The title or lockup sits with its centre between y 300 and 540, on screen 2.5 to 4 s, never with a caption group, kept inside y 240 to 1680 so the taller grid tile still shows it. Cover frame from the lockup.
- A held series title, small, top centre, for the whole episode, is allowed on the daily format only, if Jacob names the series. Everything else is off by 3 s.
- Anything a partner will run: top 269 px, bottom 672 px, sides 65 px clear.

## 9. Camera and set

- Pocket 3 on a dash or windshield mount at seated eye height (raise it, never tilt down), 70 to 90 cm from the face, never under 50 cm: at 20 mm equivalent a closer lens widens the nose and viewers mark trust down without knowing why. Loose medium close-up, chest up, both shoulders and the hands in, face a tenth left of centre, eyes on the upper-third line, a palm of headroom. Face Auto-Detect on, 4K30, no grade, white balance locked.
- Park with the sun on the passenger side; the passenger window is the key at 45 degrees, expose for the face, let the window go bright, never the driver window behind the head. Overcast beats sun; the 16:00 to 19:00 window in a Wyoming winter is already soft.
- Depth behind the shoulder: the yard through the rear or side glass, never a flat headliner.
- **Picture first (2026-11-23, look guide section 1).** "No grade" means do not add a look; it does not mean ship a bad conversion. iPhone HLG is tone-mapped at reference white (npl 203, hable), never npl 100 mobius, which lifted a direct-sun selfie until the face was red and the wall white. Before any text goes on, open a full-size frame from the first shot and one from the middle: skin natural, blacks black, sky or wall not blown. `tools/streamline.py still` renders that first frame for approval before any long render, and `lint` now flags a washed-out or crushed frame.
- Same seat, same mount, same frame every day. Chief by default; the F600 cab or the bay for the Rival check-ins. When the shop is in frame, "I work there" in the first ten seconds.

## 10. Length and shape, by job

- **Loop or meme: 6 to 20 s.** One shot, one line, the last frame rhymes with the first.
- **Skit: 45 to 70 s,** one shot, the turn on the last line, cut on it.
- **Talking head: 40 to 60 s until average watch clears 10 s for ten episodes in a row, then 60 to 120.** The account's best talking-head watch is 21.7 s and nothing proves a 65 s face hold here yet. Both 2026 datasets agree under 30 s and over 3 min lose; they disagree in the middle, and the account's own watch time settles it short for now.
- **Narrated story over footage: 60 to 120 s,** face late, every clause cut to its noun, the answer in the last ten seconds. The 20 to 40 s ban stands for this shape only.
- Nothing is padded to reach a length. End when the last line is said.
- **Correction, 2026-11-22 (Jacob, on the gratitude cuts): the band is a ceiling, not a reason to cut meaning.** He rejected a 27 s one-take that passed every rule ("you cut way too much out, I want the whole message in there") and the 42 s streamline reproduction, kept the 82 s uncut take out ("I do still need it under a minute"), and approved a 57.9 s cut with the whole message, six cuts, a checklist card as the device and the lockup "IF YOUR WEEK IS HEAVY" at 0.0. So: under a minute is the limit; the editor trims the ums, the restarts and the and-thens, never the sentences he meant; and the attention at 0.0 comes from the device and the lockup, not from dropping the slow block.

## 11. The ending and the post

- Cut on the last word of the payoff. No sign-off breath, no outro, no fade. Last frame rhymes with the first so the replay is invisible; replays count as views.
- A fixed sign-off in Jacob's words is allowed once he picks it; then the cliffhanger names tomorrow's episode. No spoken follow ask by default; it is an untested variable, run once as a Trial twin against the cliffhanger before calling it banned.
- Caption line 1 is the line a viewer would text to his buddy, written as a person type; two to four lines after it with one real number; five hashtags max; no ask. If there is no such line, it is a Story.
- Pinned comment within 60 s repeats the person type and names tomorrow. Story share with the same line. Same master to TikTok and Shorts the same day, clean file, no foreign logo.

## 13. Grading, by format

The account's own 71 reels say holding and sending are different skills, inversely related here (watch versus shares per reach at minus 0.21): talking heads are the best hook and hold (29.8 median skip, 14.8 s) and have never produced more than four shares; memes are the only format that does views and sends at once (0.56 percent median sends). So:

- Memes and loops: sends per reach, floor 1 percent, goal 2.
- Talking heads: watch share (floor 50 percent of runtime) and likes per reach (floor 4 percent), sends logged, not graded, until one clears 0.7.
- Skits and stories: skip under 40 and sends per reach over 1.
- Everything: skip rate at 48 h as the early warning, views at 7 days as the judge.
- Add `duration_s` to the Instagram pull and backfill the 71 reels; watch share cannot be argued without it.
