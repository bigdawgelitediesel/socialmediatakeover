# 1. HOOKS — the 1,300-hook study (`QTgabAQ9kCU`)

This is the densest video. He analyzed 1,300 viral Instagram reels, saved and categorized them himself, and ran a ChatGPT reading-comprehension pass over the hook text. **[E-ish: his own dataset, sample size stated, methodology partly stated, unpublished. Directional, not peer-reviewed.]**

He repeats the headline rule in `cDcCEZjikKg` as the **"negative 585 rule."**

## 1.1 The measured findings

| Finding | Number | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Average length of the most viral hooks | **7.7 words** | [E-ish] |
| Best-performing band | **5–8 words** | [E-ish] |
| Still works, upper edge | up to **12 words** | [E-ish] |
| Diminishing returns begin | **13+ words** — "the longer the hook was, the worse the video performed" | [E-ish] |
| Hooks under 4 words | rare; outliers only ("unpopular opinion", "stop scrolling") | [E-ish] |
| Reading level | **below 5th grade** average | [E-ish] |
| Negative vs positive framing | "negative hooks far outperformed their positive counterparts" | [E-ish] |

**The negative word list he gives repeatedly:** *don't, stop, avoid, cancel, stay away from, no, loss, lose, quit, crash, snapped.*

**The "drunk grandma" test** [O]: *"your marketing, your content, your videos should be so easy to understand that a drunk grandma could understand it without any extra context."* And the screenshot version: *"if you were to take a screenshot half a second into your reel and show it to a drunk grandma, would she have a pretty good understanding of what's going on?"*

**Why simple, in his words** [O]: *"it's not that all of your followers and customers are just idiots, it's that they're distracted… if your hook includes some fancy words, some vocab that's over their head, insider lingo, or an acronym that they don't already know, then they're going to be confused. And when you confuse, you lose."*

> **Direct relevance to Jake.** This is the single biggest trap in his niche. "6.7 Powerstroke," "EGT," "CP4," "DPF delete," "Class 8" are all insider lingo/acronyms. Brock's rule says the *hook* must be drunk-grandma-legible; the *body* is where the expertise goes. He names this the **curse of knowledge**: *"you forget where they're at or you forget what they don't know. You can't remember what it was like when you were first getting started."* [O]

## 1.2 The visual beats the words — his strongest repeated claim

He says this in three separate videos and it is the thing he'd tell Jake first.

*"The visual on the screen matters so much more than the words being said or the words being written… The viewer is going to comprehend the visual and they're going to take in what is on the screen before they're able to skim and read the hook."* [O]

Support he cites: MrBeast on the Colin & Samir podcast with Mark Zuckerberg, **March 27, 2025** — *"my most viewed reels on your platforms… are ones where I don't speak."* [E — real, verifiable interview. Note Brock partly misreads it: MrBeast's point was about **language gating** limiting international reach, not about visuals beating words. Brock acknowledges this mid-explanation and uses it anyway.]

Two stats he attaches, both **marketing folklore, treat as false**: "the brain processes images 60,000 times faster than words" [⚠ debunked, no source exists] and "human attention span is shorter than a goldfish — that's a fact, you can look it up" [⚠ debunked]. The "13 milliseconds to recognize an image" figure does trace to a real 2014 MIT study.

**The baseline rule:** *"the visual in your hook needs to relate to what your reel is about. If I'm a fitness coach making a talking head reel, I should not be sitting at a desk. I should not be sitting in my car. I should be in the gym… If you're a real estate agent, then you should be touring the home while you talk about the home."* [O]

> **For Jake, this is nearly free and he is probably already violating it.** A talking-head reel about turbos filmed in a truck cab or living room is "lazy B-roll." The same script filmed *with his hands on the turbo*, *under the F350*, *at the parts counter with Class 8 racking behind him* is a different post algorithmically and psychologically. He has, physically, some of the best B-roll in the niche — a semi shop and a project truck — and Brock's whole argument is that not using it wastes the hook.

**The visual checklist he gives** [O]:
- Less cluttered · higher contrast · brighter · more saturated · bigger, bolder text
- **Max three elements on screen.** He names the exact failure: *"they have a background visual… their face on the camera… a block of text above their head… and that hook is different than the words that they're saying… and then closed captions below their mouth… and then sometimes a prop or an emoji or a GIF. And so that's five, six, seven things on the screen at once."* → For Jake: **if the on-screen text hook is different from the spoken hook, that's already two competing elements. Make them the same.**
- Point with a finger or arrow to direct the eye
- **Does it make sense on mute?** *"a lot of people watch Instagram on mute."* Turn the volume off and check if the first 3 seconds still work.
- Something dynamic/changing, not static
- A problem, impending challenge or negative visual beats a positive one
- "Would someone screenshot this frame?"

**Distinctiveness questions** [O]:
- *"Have I seen this visual hook done 100 times?"* (a man holding his phone talking to camera: yes, a hundred a day)
- *"How different does it look compared to the five posts above it and the five posts below it in the feed?"*
- His own gimmick: *"every few months, I will post a reel that's literally just upside down… every single time it grabs people's attention."*

## 1.3 The 15 hook categories — verbatim, with a diesel version for each

**1. Pattern interrupt.** Break the expectation. Sudden movement, camera zoom, an effect, a dropping phone; an abrupt statement ("stop scrolling"); a visual anomaly (upside down, extreme close-up, or *"most of your reels are filmed in your home and suddenly you're out in an open field and you're 300 ft away from the camera"*); a sound effect. Caveat he gives: sound hooks fail on mute, and a too-loud pop just annoys people.
→ *Jake:* start the reel already under the truck, lens 6 inches from a cracked injector line. Or film one from 300 ft out across a Wyoming field with the F350 as the only object in frame.

**2. Contrarian.** *"These hooks go against the grain. They're the unpopular opinions or the controversial hot takes… humans are hardwired to react to threats or disagreements. And so they're going to continue watching either to hear your argument or to get some ammunition for their own argument that they're going to try to make in the comments."*
→ *Jake:* "Your EGT gauge is lying to you." / "Deleting your truck cost you money."

**3. Curiosity gap.** The Goldilocks framing, which he gives in three videos. Too wide = too vague, viewer can't see the reward. Too narrow = you gave away the answer, no reason to stay. His worked example:
- Too wide: *"This changed everything for me."*
- Too narrow: *"Creating a spreadsheet for my Pokémon cards changed everything in my collection."*
- Right: *"This one thing changed everything about my Pokémon card collection, and after 4 years, I finally finished."*
→ *Jake:* too wide — "This mistake cost me thousands." Too narrow — "Not changing your fuel filter every 10k will kill your CP4." Right — **"This $14 part is why your CP4 grenades — and nobody at the dealership will tell you."**

**4. Outcome-based.** Lead with the result. *"This reel got 2.1 million views. Here's why."* Key nuance: **use realistic numbers.** *"If I say, here's how my client got 17 billion views, a lot of my followers are going to scroll away because it's unrealistic… using realistic numbers for this is totally fine."*
→ *Jake:* "I fixed a $4,200 shop quote for $60 in my driveway."

**5. Direct address.** Call out the viewer by description. *"If you're a mom of three under three, keep watching."* *"If you're a solo entrepreneur trying to figure out Instagram, listen up."* He notes adjacent and even out-of-target people still watch to find out if it applies to them.
→ *Jake:* "If you own a 6.7 Powerstroke with over 150,000 miles, keep watching."

**6. Storytelling.** *"The one common denominator in storytelling based hooks is they use the word I. I almost quit blank last year. I was stuck at blank. I lost blank."* He cites "stories are 22 times more memorable than facts" [⚠ this is misattributed folklore — the Jennifer Aaker "22x" line has no study behind it. The advice is still sound; the stat is not.]
→ **This category is the direct match to Jake's best-ever post (15.46% engagement, personal, first-person).** It should be the default, not an occasional experiment.

**7. Hook swap.** ⚠ **See risk register, §9.** Take an existing viral clip (his examples: someone throwing toilet paper out of an airplane, a kid doing parkour about to fall), then **match cut** into your own content — *"the image on the screen at the end of the first scene matches or is super similar to what's on the screen at the beginning of the second clip."* He points at the account "Wants and Needs Brand" as the exemplar and offers a free library of 40 such clips by DM.

**8. Authority / data-backed.** *"Don't trust me, trust the data, and here's what the data says."* "According to [WHO / FDA / the White House / X study]." Purpose: *"reduce the automatic skepticism that anyone is going to have, especially when they're a stranger."*
→ *Jake's differentiator lives here.* "Ford's own TSB says…" / "The Class 8 spec for this is 3× what Ford calls for on the same part." This is the hook family where semi-parts knowledge converts into credibility with pickup owners.

**9. Bold claim.** A strong declarative statement; *"the certainty that you have in this statement is going to signal your expertise."* His warning: *"make sure that it is a hill that you are willing to die on… something that you are ready to argue with people about in the comments."*

**10. Relatable.** "When you…", "POV:", "Me when…". *"The mirror neurons in your viewers' minds fire, and they don't see you or imagine you in this scenario, they imagine themselves."*

**11. POV (literal).** Shot as if filmed by a bystander — *"a voyeuristic effect… whatever is about to happen you weren't supposed to know."* Technique: **fake Snapchat-style text overlay**, then match-cut into the real content. He names Citizen the Artist.
→ *Jake:* dashcam/bystander framing of something going wrong in the shop yard.

**12. Urgency / time-bound.** *"Before you go to the gym today, do this."* "In 2026…" "Before your head hits the pillow tonight…"
→ *Jake:* "Before it drops below zero this week, do this to your diesel." (Wyoming winter is a permanent, renewable urgency engine — cold-start, gelling, block heaters, DEF freeze.)

**13. List.** "3 ways to…", "5 foods to avoid…". *"These make your audience feel like there is structured value and predictable outcomes… people know what they're going to get."*

**14. Comparison.** Show a difference rather than describe it. "Me before I did X." Or a **split screen**, top vs bottom — *"it's a visually interesting break-the-norm style hook because that's not what most reels are."*

**15. Identity.** Distinct from #5: not demographics, but self-image. *"Good snowboarders always do this. Lazy moms start their day like this. Disciplined entrepreneurs never do…"* Mechanism: *"people will keep watching to either make sure that they align with the positive trait or make sure that they don't align with the negative trait."*
→ *Jake:* "Guys who actually work their trucks never do this." Powerful in this niche, where "real work truck vs. mall crawler" is a live identity line.

# 2. SHARES / SENDS — Jake's worst metric

Almost all of this is from `cDcCEZjikKg` plus tricks 16–20 of `nrkeLfjGZvM` and lesson 1 of `MX-Emk6vkE4`.

## 2.1 The core psychological claim

*"People don't share your content because of what you said. They share your content because of what it says about them."* [O — but this is the most actionable idea in the set.]

Expanded: *"Let's say I see your post and I think it's super funny and so I send it to my wife. I am sharing it with her to signal to her that I am funny or that I am the source of the funny material."*

The highest form, in his framing: *"tap into the things that they want to say, but that they'll never say out loud… so when you post saying those things, they can then share your post to amplify your voice. It's a way that they can say it without directly saying it."*

His own worked example is instructive: he identified that his audience (small business owners) universally resents *lack of support from friends and family*, started making posts saying that out loud, and they shared them.

> **The equivalent for Jake.** What does a diesel owner in a small town believe but not say? Candidates: that the dealership is upselling him; that most "truck guys" online have never worked; that he can't afford to be down a day; that his truck is judged by people who've never towed anything; that the EPA/emissions stuff is a tax on working people. Pick the ones Jake actually believes — Brock's §3 rule is that faked conviction is detectable.

## 2.2 Ask for the share — and name who and why

*"The most basic tip, and I know this is elementary, but it's funny how many people don't do this, is to ask for shares."* [O]

**The upgrade:** *"don't just say share this post, but tell them who to share it with and maybe why they should share it."* He breaks it into **who** + **why**:
- "Send this to your boyfriend (who) who needs to learn how to take better selfies of you (why)."
- "Send this to yourself (who) so that it's easier for you to find in the future (why)."
- "Share this to your story (who) if you agree (why)."

**The "send it to me" trick** [O]: he tells people to send the reel *back to him*. *"It counts as a share. It doesn't require them to think about who they're sending it to… But that is a signal to the Instagram algorithm."*
→ For Jake this is unusually clean: "Send this back to me and I'll tell you what your truck's actual problem is." It converts a share into a DM conversation, which is also the fastest route to reach→follow.

**Best variant in the whole set — the `_pkUQqA2w3I` immediate call-out.** Open the reel with "Send this to ___":
*"If someone already is that person, they don't have to send it to anyone, they're just naturally going to keep watching themselves. But if someone isn't that intended viewer, they're not just told to continue scrolling — they're actually going to send it to that intended viewer. This gets you more shares, and it doesn't just get you more views, it gets you better viewers."*
→ *Jake:* "Send this to the buddy who's about to buy a 6.7 with 200k on it." / "Send this to whoever told you to delete it." This is a hook and a share CTA in the same 8 words, and it self-qualifies the audience. **Highest-leverage single tactic here.**

## 2.3 The SHARE method — what a shareable post contains

- **S — Simple.** *"Very rarely are people sharing posts that are complex, confusing, and overwhelming."*
- **H — Hook.**
- **A — Authenticity.** His only genuinely practical definition of the word: ***"Reduce the time between idea and execution."*** *"Every single minute that you spend refining, trimming, filtering, doing all that crap, the less authentic it is becoming, the more literally filtered it is becoming… If that means literally just whipping out your phone, hitting record in your car, and then pressing post without any editing at all, including the bloopers and the outtakes, then that will be more authentic and that is worth it."*
  → **This is the single best piece of advice in 314KB of transcript for a one-man operation with a full-time job.** It converts Jake's biggest constraint (no editing time) into the thing that's supposed to perform.
- **R — Relatable, via specificity.** *"Relatability comes from specificity."* His worked contrast: "I woke up this morning and I was so tired" (everyone relates, nobody cares) vs. *"this morning when my alarm went off, I punched my phone like I was the Incredible Hulk and before I could even open my eyes, my alerts were already going off… and then before my feet even touched the floor, I realized I had let out three loud, deep groaning sighs."* The principle: *"it will allow 30% of your viewers to relate to you so flipping hard that they will share your reel because of it."*
  → *Jake:* not "diesels are expensive." Instead: the exact moment at 5:40am at −14° when it cranks, catches, and dies, and what he said out loud.
- **E — Entertainment, not education.** *"Pure education, while it does have value, it's likely not going to get shared… Are there plenty of valuable textbooks at my local library? Yeah. But have any of them been checked out in the last decade?"*

## 2.4 The TRIBE categories — what shareable posts *are*

- **T — Timely.** Breaking news, trends, pop culture. He doesn't expect you to break news: *"take the things that are newsworthy… and add your two cents. Do a green screen video and talk about the news, break it down and share your own opinion."* Sub-tactic he names **SNAP — niche adjacent post**: take a pop-culture moment and bend it to the niche.
  → *Jake:* new emissions rules, a Ford TSB or recall, fuel price moves, a new truck launch, a viral towing fail. Green-screen reaction is ~3 minutes of work and is the cheapest repeatable format he has.
- **R — Relatable.** Memes, jokes, self-deprecating content.
- **I — Inspirational.** Personal triumphs and struggles, things overcome. → The build itself is this, if framed as struggle rather than spec sheet.
- **B — Beckoning.** Calling on the community for help / a cause. His examples: the ALS ice bucket challenge (*"a very strong visual hook, and then tag three friends, which basically means share it with three friends"*), Kylie Jenner's "make Instagram Instagram again" — *"she spoke up as a leader about something that a lot of people were feeling, but no one had a clear way to say it."* He's explicit this is rare: *"you probably aren't even going to do them every month."*
- **E — Edgy.** Hot takes, unpopular opinions. *"You're not calling out an individual person. You're calling out an idea… there is a silent group of people in your audience who is thinking the exact same thing."*

## 2.5 Share psychology, tricks 16–20

- **16. Identity signaling** — *"your reel has to articulate a view or a belief better than your audience could say it themselves, or you have to be willing to say something that your audience normally wouldn't be willing to say themselves."*
- **17. Say it outright** — "Send this to…" (as above)
- **18. Timeliness** — sharing timely content is itself a signal: *"it's their own way of saying, hey, I'm in the know."*
- **19. In-group language** — slang, references and terms insiders know and outsiders don't. *"It creates kind of like an us versus them, insiders versus outsiders feeling."*
  → ⚠ **Tension with §1.1.** He tells you to write hooks at a 5th-grade level with no insider lingo, then tells you in-group language drives shares. **Resolution for Jake: plain-English hook, in-group language in the body and the caption.** The jargon is the payoff, not the entry fee.
- **20. Controversy** — *"the more controversial you are, usually the more viral you're going to be… but that can also come with a pretty significant cost."* His setup phrases: "This might be controversial, but…" / "Unpopular opinion…" / "I might get cancelled for saying this, but…"

## 2.6 The one share stat, and why not to trust it

*"One share on average equals 400 additional views."* (`nrkeLfjGZvM`) **[O — no source given, appears nowhere else in the 12 videos, and is structurally suspect: Mosseri's named signal is sends *per reach*, a ratio, so a share cannot have a fixed view-multiplier independent of reach.]** Do not plan against this number.

# 3. RETENTION — the Attention Formula (`igV6Ll8c7lI`) and tricks 6–10

Jake is at 6.8s against a stated platform average of 8.5s. This section is where that moves.

## 3.1 The 3 S's

**S1 — STRUCTURE.** His argument: traditional storytelling arcs don't fit a reel. *"Instead, we're just going to be opening the door to create curiosity and then closing the door, opening and closing over and over again."*

The five-step build, which is a genuinely usable scripting system:
1. Pick a **specific** topic (not "fitness" → "learning to snowboard")
2. **Brain-dump every point** you could make about it
3. **Circle the three most non-obvious points**
4. **Reverse-engineer the setup** each one needs — *"since these were the three most non-obvious takeaways, maybe the setup is going to be what most people think, which is probably the opposite"*
5. **Stack them in ascending order** of complexity, surprisingness, or importance

His finished 30-second output: *"Don't try snowboarding until you watch this video. Number one, I don't care what you wear, just don't wear those dumb butt pads… Number two, your friends are not coaches… Number three, falling is a good thing. Falling is not failing, it's how you learn."*

→ **This is the template for Jake's 4 weekly talking-heads.** Topic: "changing your fuel filter." Non-obvious points: the one everyone gets wrong, the one the manual is wrong about, the one that's actually fine to ignore. Set up each with the belief it overturns.

**S2 — PSYCHOLOGY.** Four keys: hook (visual > words, colors, rule of three), curiosity gap, **anticipation**, payoff.

Anticipation tactics [O]:
- **Vary format between posts** — same style every time gets boring
- **Vary sentence length** — *"there can be a real music in incorporating shorter sentences, sentences that have a punch… and then sentences that might be a little bit longer, a little bit wordier"*
- Build in an **unexpected outcome or plot twist**, so viewers learn they never know how it ends

Payoff: *"your hook is making a promise in the minds of the viewers. And by the end of the content, that promise needs to be paid off."* Done repeatedly it becomes *"a Pavlovian response… they learn to trust you."*

**S3 — SWAPS.** Seven word-level edits, all cheap:
1. **Name your frameworks.** He calls this "mystify the mundane." → *Jake has an obvious one: a named pre-winter checklist, a named "parts counter rule."*
2. **Delete weak language.** Verbatim substitutions he gives: "I think" → "I've found" / "here's what works" / "here's what happens when". "Maybe you could" → "try this" / "do this instead" / "start here". "This might" → "this will" / "this fixes" / "this is how you". **And: *"instead of saying just, remove the word just completely. And the same goes for kind of, sort of, a little, maybe, hopefully — delete them."***
3. **Use their words, not yours.** His own case: renamed a lead magnet from "maximum engagement checklist" to "maximum **views** checklist" — same file — and *"our downloads went through the roof,"* because *"no one was ever using the word engagement. Everyone was talking about views."*
   → *Jake: owners don't search "EGT management," they search "why does my truck smoke."*
4. **Negative words over positive counterparts.** "Don't try snowboarding until you watch this" > "Three things to keep in mind before you learn to snowboard."
5. **Re-hook mid-video.** *"But here's the thing everyone misses." / "This is where things get interesting." / "This is where everyone messes up."*
6. **Add contrast — the word "but."** *"Do you want to know what the most powerful word in retention is? It's the word but. Using the word but introduces conflict and conflict holds attention."* The drill: *"see if you could add the word therefore or but to the beginning of every sentence"* in your script. If you can't, the script is exposition. **[This is a lift of the South Park "but/therefore" rule, uncredited — but it's real and it works.]**
7. **Hook swap** — ⚠ see §9.

## 3.2 Retention mechanics, tricks 6–10

- **6. Zeigarnik effect** — people fixate on unfinished tasks. Implementation: *"there are three tips you need to know… and I've saved the best for last."*
- **7. The two-second rule** — cut every ~2 seconds. **His method is perfect for a one-man crew with no editing time:** *"I will film myself, often with my phone, saying my hook. Then I will stop recording, I'll switch to my left hand. This will change up what's in my background, it'll be a slightly new angle, and then I'll say my second sentence… This way I don't have to remember my entire script. I also don't have to try to be perfect and read off a teleprompter. It keeps my background dynamic."* One clip per sentence, hand-switching between takes. Zero edit-suite time.
- **8. Visual countdown** — an on-screen timer or a 1-through-5 list the eye wants completed.
- **9. Contrast framing** — before/after, problem/solution, old/new, split screen (*"the top half maybe in black and white… the bottom half in color"*).
- **10. Micro-cliffhangers** — don't end a segment cleanly, tease into the next. His worked line: *"I stopped saying no to my puppy, and what I replaced it with changed everything. But first, you need to understand why I stopped saying no."*
  → *Jake:* "I stopped using OEM filters, and what I switched to saved me $900 a year. But first you need to see what the old one did to my injectors."

## 3.3 Emotional triggers, tricks 11–15

- **11. Social proof** — testimonials, case studies, actual numbers.
- **12. Loss aversion** — *"losing something feels roughly twice as bad as getting something of equal value."* Reframe benefits as avoided losses. → "Every mile you run it like this costs you $X" beats "here's how to get better economy."
- **13. Relatability shock** — a vulnerable confession. *"Vulnerability makes you more trustworthy and it makes your followers feel less alone,"* and it opens a loop. → Jake admitting a mistake he made on his own truck is both this and hook category 6.
- **14. Righteous anger** — *"calling out some sort of unfair norm that your audience has often been quietly resenting."* His guardrail: *"I don't want you to make this a person… much more effective and much safer to make it a construct or an idea."*
- **15. Aspirational mirroring** — show the identity they want. *"People don't just buy products, they buy a futuristic idealized better version of themselves."*

## 3.4 Engagement mechanics, tricks 21–25

- **21. Deliberate error** ⚠ — a planted typo or odd pronunciation to bait corrections. He does a careful non-denial (*"I have never intentionally made a typo"*) then explains exactly how to do it. **This is engagement bait; Meta's published policy demotes it. See §9.**
- **22. Binary questions** — this-or-that, A/B, yes/no. Easy to answer. → Excellent for Jake: "Ford or Cummins." "Delete or keep it stock." "Gauge pod or leave the dash alone."
- **23. Fill-in-the-blank** — his most viral post of the year was *a plain white background* reading "Fill in the blank: I unfollow people when ___." → *Jake:* "I knew my mechanic was ripping me off when ___."
- **24. Reciprocity** — over-deliver value first, ask second.
- **25. Curiosity-driven CTA** — give the ask a reason: *"Follow for part two." / "Save this reel, and if it gets over 100 saves, I will post a follow-up."*

# 4. POST FORMATS — all 22 ideas, named and executable

## 4.1 The "13 posts" (`ouJ2bADHSug`)

1. **Gold Star Behavior** (carousel or reel). Photo/video of yourself in-niche + big gold text "GOLD STAR BEHAVIOR" + gold stars placed on screen, each labeled with a behavior you want to reward. *"The viewers are not only likely to engage, they're also likely to share the reel, especially if they're trying to claim one of these gold stars."* Film hands-free so there's frame space for the stars. → *Jake:* gold stars for "greases the fifth wheel before it squeaks," "keeps receipts in the glovebox," "lets it idle down after a pull."
2. **Customer Callout** (carousel). Set it up to look like you're calling someone out for an unpaid invoice or theft — *"intentionally vague, or maybe even a little bit misleading language"* — then the swipe reveals it's a testimonial/case study. → *Jake:* a guy at the counter he actually helped.
3. **[Lyrics] To The Music carousel.** Add music from Instagram's library at post time, pre-plan the song, put one line of lyric per slide. Two effects he claims: viewers turn the volume up, and they swipe to stay with the lyrics. **He also claims adding music is what makes a carousel eligible to appear on the Reels tab** [O, unverified].

# 5. The daily challenge play (`O_rsTEiUSrU`)

His single biggest-claimed growth mechanism. Treat the case studies as survivorship-selected marketing [O]; the design rules are still sound.

**Three formats:**
1. **"Day X of ___ every day until [goal]"** — DoorDashing for a diamond ring (+400k); finger-surfing until my business blows up (+23k); 20-min daily workouts until I get jacked (+180k)
2. **"___ for every new follower"** — *"this second template has more viral potential because getting someone to follow you is directly baked into the challenge — when they follow you, it triggers one additional action that you have to take."* Run a mile per follower (+2M); one push-up per follower
3. **Per-follower but not daily** — pet the dog N times, add an eyeball to a mural per follower (+109k in 3 weeks), a sticky note per follower on a pride-flag mural (+20k)

**The design rules [O]:**
- **Must be niche-related.** *"If your niche has nothing to do with dogs, that would be a really dumb challenge… you're going to go viral in the wrong way. You're going to end up with a bunch of followers who don't actually care about your business."* ← This is precisely Jake's existing failure mode: 290k views, 17 followers.
- **It has to actually be hard.** *"The more challenging it is, the more likely people are to follow."*
- **Commit long.** Day 1 is exciting, day 5 less, day 28 much less. *"Commit to at least 90 days… 100 days… an entire year before you decide to give up or pivot."* Milestones re-ignite it: *"every time I hit one of those, people are like, oh my gosh, you're still doing it."*
- **No time-bound cap.** Use a follower or outcome goal, or none. *"If I follow you on day 33 and you're doing it for 50 days, that means I'm only going to be able to follow you for 17 days… it disincentivizes."*
- **Add curiosity / a journey.** Gage works because he started at 400+ lb; the transformation is the content.
- **Pivoting is allowed** if the premise is wrong (he pivoted twice), not if it's merely slow.
- Secondary use: *"if you're someone who just struggles to stay consistent, coming up with a daily challenge will help, because then every single day you don't have to come up with a new post idea."* He guarantees himself one post/day this way.
- ⚠ **You can also key it to shares** — *"you can say that you're going to do a push-up for every single person who shares this post."* **For Jake, whose worst metric is shares/reach, this is the version to consider.** He also rotates the CTA daily *"just to keep people on their toes and make it so that you actually have to watch the reel to hear what the call to action is."*

**Feasibility note:** every one of his headline examples is either a full-time creator or a physical-transformation story. Jake works full time. The **consistency** version is realistic ("Day X of fixing something on Chief with parts I can get in Wyoming"); the **virality** version is a second job.

# 8. THE 8 MISTAKES (`LgotQKHJ0ok`) — all of them

**1. Deleting or archiving posts.** Three reasons [O]: (a) you destroy the diagnostic data — *"you're able to see the skip rate, which helps you determine was the hook good, at what point were people falling off"* — **and the algorithm loses it too**, so it can never learn who you're for; (b) it means you're playing for vanity metrics and will burn out — *"if you're a baseball player who is constantly overanalyzing every single time that you strike out, you're going to be too scared to even step into the batter's box"*; (c) **posts take off days, weeks or months later** — *"as soon as you delete it, you literally cannot get any more views on it."* He adds a health metric: **20–40% of your posts should be flopping**, because that's evidence you're experimenting.

**2. Focusing on the algorithm instead of the audience.** *"Every single time you think about the word algorithm, I want you to instead replace it with the word audience."* His canonical example of algorithm-first thinking is the **7-second B-roll + "read the caption" reel**: clickbait text, deliberately short video, long caption, so the video loops while you read. *"For a while the algorithm was like, wow, this video had a 400% play rate… But the algorithm has adapted."* Two failure modes: tricks don't last, **and the audience hates it** — *"they're annoyed, or they intentionally scroll, or they even block people who say 'read the caption.'"* The Short `Ek0jZ85c6rc` is the same point: *"Reels that have a dramatic hook and then just say 'read the caption' are the Instagram equivalent of clickbait… can we all agree to stop valuing the algorithm over our audience?"*

**3. Inconsistency — "in my opinion the number one reason that people are not growing."** Two kinds:
- **Frequency.** *"There is a direct correlation between feed frequency and the average growth rate."* Daily > every other day [O, "the studies show" — no study named]. His diagnosis: *"Most people are already creating content that is of the level of quality necessary to see results. What they need to do is pull the lever of quantity."*
- **Niche.** ⚠ **This one matters most for Jake.** His worked example is exactly Jake's risk: a rock-climbing account posts one meal-prep reel, it goes viral, brings in food followers, and now *"every time you post about rock climbing, a big chunk of your audience just doesn't care and they don't engage. And then when you go back to meal prepping, all of your original rock climbing followers don't care."*
  → **This is a mechanism for Jake's 0.037% conversion.** One reel at 260,008 views — 90% of lifetime views — pulled in an audience that may not be diesel people at all, and every subsequent build post is served first to them.

**4. Mono-posting** — only ever making one type of post. *"Don't be a one-trick pony."* Vary reels/carousels/photos, and vary reel *formats* (talking head, yapping, B-roll, cinematic, clone).
→ Jake's stated plan — 4 talking-heads + 2 "showing what's going on" — is two formats. Per Brock that's near-mono. Cheap variants that cost nothing: whiteboard, the actual part in hand, caveman talk, bad/better/best, green-screen reaction.

**5. Confusing "valuable" with "educational."** *"All of your posts should be valuable, but not all of your posts need to have education as the main purpose… most people are scrolling on social media not to learn, but to be entertained."* Fix: keep the education, change the container (the two-phones format is his example).

**6. Chasing hacks.** *"Oftentimes they reward the person who's posting about the hack more than the person who's actually going to be using the hack, which is you."* Five he debunks: hashtags; "you can't edit a caption after posting" (false — you can, no penalty); a universal best time to post (false); **trending audios** — *"today it's more like putting lipstick on a pig… a trending audio cannot save a bad post"*, pick audio that fits the emotional register instead; "you can't post and ghost" (false — *"whether you post and you stay super engaged, or you post and then you chuck your phone in a river, the post will perform based off how engaging the post is, not how engaged you are"*).

**7. Reinventing the wheel.** His picture of the default grind: *"Every single day they wake up with no posts ready. So that day they have to get inspired, come up with the post, script it, plan it, film it, edit it, write the caption, and then post it. Eight steps every single day."* Three fixes:
- **Re-upload your hits** at **90+ days**. *"All of my best content on Instagram gets re-uploaded over and over again until it eventually stops performing."* The bigger the hit, the longer you wait. ⚠ *See §9 on repost volume.*
- **Remake your flops.** *"Just because a post flopped, it wasn't necessarily an issue with the topic… Maybe there's a different hook we can put at the beginning of the story."* Same content, new format/hook.
- **Borrow formats that are working for others.** Study the *visual container*, not the audio or the hook. *"Take your fuel — your content, your ideas, your tips — and simply put it into an already existing car."*

**8. Not understanding Stories.** He says the Stories algorithm prioritizes **three things: watch time, tap-backs, and DM replies** — *"direct messages, the most important of all"* [O, no source]. Then three Story mistakes:
- **Long talking-head video stories** — people bail.
- **Sharing your own feed posts to your story.** Real trade-off, stated honestly: it *does* boost the feed post, but *"if your goal is to maximize your story views, don't share anything to your stories from your feed."* His logic: the viewer either taps forward (uninterested) or taps out to the post (interested) — either way no watch time, no tap-back, no reply.
- **The link sticker** — same mechanism, viewer leaves or skips.
→ *For Jake, the useful inversion:* Stories optimized for **DM replies** (polls, questions, "what's this noise?", "guess what failed") build the 1:1 conversations Brock says are the best source of audience insight (§2.1). At 215 followers, DMs are worth more than story views.
